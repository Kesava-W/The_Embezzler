# Chapter Five — Mauritius

By the fourth week, the cohort had sorted itself into the usual smaller shapes a class does once the seating charts stop mattering, and Ashwin had landed, without much effort on his part, in one of the better ones.

It was Neha - one of the two others from India, sharp-tongued in a way that made the long lectures bearable - who suggested the beach that Saturday, mostly to escape a group assignment nobody had started. Kwame, the Kenyan transfer, was in before she'd finished the sentence. Mrudula came because Neha threatened to report her missing to the course coordinator if she didn't, which was not a threat Mrudula seemed to take seriously so much as an excuse she was glad to be given. Marc, a quiet Creole-Mauritian classmate who sat in the front row and had, until that Saturday, said perhaps eleven words to any of them combined, turned out to be the one with a car, which was reason enough for the group to adopt him on the spot and considerably more warmly than his eleven words had so far earned him. Five of them in the end, three of them squeezed into the back of Marc's ageing Corolla and two onto the 57 outside the Quatre Bornes bus station on a Saturday morning, the kind of bus that filled up fast and stayed that way the whole route out to the coast.

Flic en Flac on a Saturday was already busy by the time they got off - families with cool boxes staking out the good shade under the filao trees, a group of local boys playing football along the tideline with the tide as the only referee, the long white curve of the beach doing exactly what a postcard promised it would. Nobody in the group had planned anything beyond being there. That turned out to be enough.

They found a strip of sand past the crowd, close enough to the water that the tide kept nearly reaching their bags, and did nothing in particular for most of the afternoon that would have made a story on its own - swimming out further than was sensible and getting shouted back in by a lifeguard's whistle, a running debate about which of them would sink first if a shark actually turned up, Kwame attempting and failing spectacularly to bodysurf a wave that had looked, from the beach, considerably smaller than it turned out to be. Marc, it emerged somewhere around the second hour, had eleven words for strangers and considerably more than that for people he'd decided he liked, and spent a happy twenty minutes trying to teach Neha to say a full sentence in Kreol that she mangled so badly even the boys playing football further down the beach turned to look. Ashwin lost a good half hour trying to teach Neha to skim stones and gave up only when she pointed out, not unreasonably, that he wasn't very good at it either.

A woman came past on a bicycle mid-afternoon with a glass case strapped to the back, dholl puri wrapped in paper, gâteaux piment still warm from the oil, and the five of them bought more than they could reasonably eat and ate it anyway, sitting in the sand with chilli on their fingers, arguing about whether the tamarind chutney or the coriander one was better and never settling it - Marc, when finally consulted as the only actual Mauritian in the group, refused to take a side at all, on the grounds that any opinion he gave would follow him for the rest of the degree.

By evening someone had produced a speaker from a beach bag and a case of Phoenix, and further down the sand a family had started up something closer to sega without much ceremony about it - a drum, a triangle, someone's grandmother dancing better than any of the young men trying to keep up with her, a small crowd gathering the way small crowds do around anything with rhythm in it. Nobody in Ashwin's group was quite drunk, not really, but the sun had done most of that work anyway, and by the time the sky over the water went the deep orange-pink that the west coast did better than anywhere Ashwin had lived before, he'd laughed more in one afternoon than he could remember laughing in months.

"You're different today," Mrudula said at one point, not looking at him, watching the water instead. "Lighter."

"Different how?"

"I don't know. Just - today you're actually here." She said it the way she'd said most things to him so far, lightly, already half turning it into a joke before he could make anything of it. "Anyway. Don't let it go to your head."

He didn't have an answer ready for that, so he didn't try to find one, and the moment passed the way most of that afternoon's moments did - easily, without needing to be held onto.

They caught the last comfortable bus back, Marc having long since been talked into staying for the sega rather than driving anyone home early, salt-skinned and sun-tired, and split off toward their own buildings from the Quatre Bornes stop with the loose, unfinished goodbyes of people who knew they'd see each other Monday.

The parcel was waiting outside his door when he got in, propped the same way it always was, MERIDIAN EXPRESS printed across a label gone slightly soft at one corner from the day's heat. He set his bag down first, kicked off shoes still half full of sand he'd deal with in the morning. Then he brought the parcel to the table and opened it the way he always did - unhurried, each layer set aside rather than torn through, a tin of something from his mother's kitchen, a folded letter he hadn't asked for, each item turned once in his hands and set down in its row before he moved on to the next.

Sunburnt, salt still drying on his shoulders, laughing twenty minutes earlier at nothing in particular - and at the table, for the length of time it took to work through the parcel, none of that showed on his face at all.
