# Chapter Four — India

Ravish ran Rumi's Charity out of a farmhouse in Chhattarpur that had never, in nine years, seen anything that resembled a beneficiary.

The board existed on paper. The three annual reports filed with the Ministry existed on paper too, audited, signed, technically in order. What existed off paper was the rest of it - a driver kept on the NGO's payroll who spent his days idling outside Ravish's gate rather than any project site, playing rummy on his phone with two other drivers from neighbouring farmhouses who understood the arrangement without anyone needing to explain it; a "field coordinator" who was in fact Ravish's brother-in-law and had never left Delhi, and whose quarterly progress reports on a watershed project in rural Bundelkhand were, by now, something of a private joke among the two accountants who had to format them for filing; a farmhouse lawn that hosted more birthday parties than field visits, the marquee company on speed-dial the way a functioning NGO's field office might have been. Fifteen members of staff drew salaries from an organisation that, on the last count anyone bothered to make, ran one functioning programme and eleven that existed mainly in the grant proposal that had first brought the money in.

The money itself sat where the law required it to sit - the FCRA account, State Bank of India, Sansad Marg, the designated port of entry through which every rupee of foreign contribution to Rumi's Charity was required by statute to pass before it went anywhere else. Fifteen crore, more or less, at any given time. Ravish kept the administrative spend hovering just under the legal ceiling, twenty per cent, near enough to the line to draw a raised eyebrow from an auditor with nothing better to do and never once over it. He had learned, over nine years, exactly how much fiefdom the law allowed him, and had built his life to the very edge of it, and would have told you, if asked, and had told several journalists over the years in almost these words, that he had never once broken a rule - only found, patiently, every last centimetre of the room the rules actually left him.

The call came in a little after four in the afternoon, while he was on the lawn in a cane chair, going through the day's second whisky with the unhurried confidence of a man who had never had a Tuesday go against him.

His assistant brought the tablet out. "Sir, video call. Number's blocked, but it says urgent."

He answered without moving from the chair, not even bothering to sit up properly, the phone propped against the ice bucket so both his hands stayed free for the glass.

The face on the screen was a smear - not blacked out, not a filter card, just deliberately, competently blurred, the outline of a head and shoulders with nothing in it a person could describe to anyone afterwards. The voice, when it came, had been run through something that flattened it to a register no human throat produced, evenly paced, unhurried in a way that should have told him more than it did.

"Mr Ravish. We are calling regarding certain activities of Rumi's Charity that are prejudicial to the country's interest. You are advised to cease these activities and cooperate with an audit of your foreign contributions, with immediate effect."

Ravish laughed before the sentence had finished landing - a short, comfortable sound, the sound of a man who had heard versions of this before, from real ministries, with real letterheads, and had outlasted every one of them. "Which department is this. Give me your name and designation, na, or hang up."

"This is not a department call, Mr Ravish."

"Then what is it." He set the whisky down, more amused now than annoyed. "Some NGO-wallah jealous of my grant? Some journalist with a fake voice, trying for a story? I have handled bigger people than whoever is sitting behind that blur. Fifteen years I am running this organisation, nobody has touched one rupee of what is mine -"

"That will not remain true past this call."

"Is it." He was almost enjoying himself now, leaning forward into the tablet's camera the way a man leans into an argument he expects to win. "You know how many times I have had this exact conversation? Income tax, ED, one enthusiastic fellow from the Ministry who lasted four months before someone senior explained to him how things actually work. You people think you can frighten me with a bad voice and a blurred face? Go and do something useful with your life. I built this from nothing. Nobody - not government, not the department, not some coward hiding his face - is going to tell me what to do with -"

He didn't finish it.

On the second screen beside him - the laptop he kept open on the garden table more from habit than need, the NGO's net banking session still logged in from that morning's transfer - the balance changed.

Fifteen crore, eleven lakh, some odd change. Then twelve crore. Then eight. The number was falling in increments too fast to be a person moving it by hand, too smooth to be a glitch, a column of digits sliding down the screen the way water finds a drain, and Ravish's laugh had already died somewhere in his throat by the time it reached zero.

He didn't call anyone. He tried, later - a bank official, his chartered accountant, a lawyer he kept on retainer for exactly this kind of emergency - but the calls came in the wrong order, to the wrong people, ten minutes too late to matter, because for the first ninety seconds after the number hit zero, Ravish did not do anything except stand up from the cane chair and scream, once, the kind of sound that carried across the lawn and brought two members of the household staff to the veranda door before they thought better of coming any closer. One of them, a young woman who'd worked the kitchen for six years and had, in a rare unguarded moment two Diwalis back, been given a bonus large enough to put her younger brother through a diploma course, hovered at the door a second longer than the other before she too withdrew, unsure, even years later, whether what she'd felt in that moment had been fear of him or something closer to pity.

The tablet, when someone finally thought to check, had already gone dark. No missed call logged. No number to trace.

He did not sleep that night, by his driver's later account - the study light had still been on past two, and at some point past midnight the driver, still awake himself in the servants' quarters, had heard what might have been Ravish's voice, low, talking to no one anyone could identify, through a wall too thick to make out the words. What he did between the call and the morning, nobody could say with any certainty, and nobody who worked for him was in a position, or in a state, to ask.

It was the housekeeper who found him, a little after seven, in the study with the curtains still drawn. She would tell the police, days later, that she had thought at first he was only asleep at the desk - it was not the first time - and that it had taken her a full minute of standing in the doorway, waiting for a stillness to become explicable, before she understood that this time it would not. She noticed, and would remember noticing, that the whisky glass from the afternoon before was still on the garden table outside, untouched since, the ice long since melted into a thin ring of water around its base.

The bottle of sleeping tablets was on the desk beside his hand, empty, the strip torn through every last blister. Nobody in that house, then or after, could have told you with any confidence whether he had reached for it out of the private arithmetic of a ruined man, or whether the hand that emptied it had not, in the end, been entirely his own. The police report would call it likely suicide, financial distress noted as a contributing factor, and leave the rest of the sentence unwritten, because there was nothing further on the desk, or in the room, or in the fifteen crores that no longer existed anywhere a bank could find them, to write it with.
