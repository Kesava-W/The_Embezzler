# Chapter Twenty-One — India

Mrudula was asleep by eleven, the way she'd been most nights since the wedding, worn out by a day that had asked nothing extraordinary of her and somehow taken everything anyway. Ashwin waited until her breathing had settled into the particular rhythm he'd learned to read without meaning to, then dressed in the dark and let himself out of a house that never once woke to notice. The street outside was empty at that hour except for a stray dog working its way along the gutter, entirely uninterested in him.

The dhaba sat on the outskirts of the city, past the last of the streetlights that still worked, a tin-roofed place that stayed open for the truck traffic and didn't ask questions of anyone else who wandered in at that hour. A generator hummed somewhere behind it, powering a single bare bulb over the counter and nothing much else. Two men sat alone in the dhaba at that hour, and for a half-second too long Ashwin found himself walking toward the wrong one - a driver on a break, going by the uniform shirt hung over the back of his chair, who glanced up with mild, unbothered curiosity at a stranger slowing near his table before Ashwin corrected course toward the man at the back, unremarkable in every direction, the kind of face a person forgets within a day of meeting it, a half-finished cup of tea in front of him and nothing else on the table to suggest he was anything other than a man waiting for a late bus that wasn't coming. No file, no phone visible, nothing that belonged to the version of this scene Ashwin might once have imagined, years ago, before he'd learned what tradecraft actually looked like - and, apparently, nothing that made him any easier to identify correctly on the first try either.

"Sit," Satish Dubey said, not looking up from the tea. "You're not late. I was early."

Ashwin sat. The bench was uneven under him, one leg shorter than the others, and he found himself absurdly noting it - the kind of detail a mind reaches for when it doesn't yet know what register the conversation ahead is going to take.

"It's done," Satish said, once the tea-stall boy had come and gone with a second cup nobody was going to drink, setting it down without being asked and vanishing again without comment, clearly used to serving men who talked in half-sentences at this hour. "Formally, as of this morning. Operation NGO is closed."

"The money."

"Six hundred and twenty-two crore, all of it accounted for, all of it sitting exactly where it's supposed to be sitting." Satish said it the way a man reads out a balance sheet he's already checked twice and has no further opinion about. "You did what you were brought in to do. Cleanly, on schedule, with a level of technical execution I'm told the department is still writing up as a case study nobody outside three offices will ever be allowed to read."

"That's meant to be a compliment."

"It's meant to be accurate." A small, dry note, the closest thing to warmth Satish's voice seemed built to allow. "You were recruited for one reason, Ashwin. Not your charm, not your patience, God knows not your MBA. You could move through a network faster and cleaner than anyone the department had on file, and every account this operation ever touched went exactly where it was supposed to go because of that, start to finish, without a single trace anyone outside our own people has managed to follow back to source."

Ashwin turned the untouched second cup of tea slowly on the table, not drinking it, watching the small ring of condensation it left behind, watching it grow and shrink as he turned the cup.

"I keep thinking about the version of this where I say no," he said. "Where I stay the person I was before the department found me. Some nights that person feels like someone I made up."

"He didn't exist for very long before we found him either." Satish said it without unkindness, simply stating a fact he assumed Ashwin already knew about himself. "You were a software engineer who happened to be extraordinary at the wrong kind of thing, in exactly the year we needed someone extraordinary at it. That's not fate. That's a recruitment file. Don't build more mythology around it than that."

There was a silence then, comfortable in the way silences only get between two people who've already said the parts of a conversation that needed saying and are simply letting the rest of it settle. A truck ground its gears somewhere out on the highway, the sound rising and fading without either of them acknowledging it.

*

He remembered it that way, mostly, in the flashes that came back to him sometimes late at night, months into the marriage, in the version of himself the MBA cover had never once let anyone in Mauritius see - the small flat, the desk cleared of the textbooks he'd never seriously opened, three screens throwing blue light across a room at two in the morning while a country's worth of foreign-funded accounts moved, one by one, exactly where he needed them to go. No music. No breaks. Just the particular, total focus of a man who had, for the length of each session, stopped being anyone's classmate, anyone's boyfriend, anyone's cover story at all - stopped, for a few hours most nights, being anyone but the one thing the department had actually recruited. He never remembered, in those flashes, what time he finally stopped each night, only the grey hour of exhaustion afterward, and the careful, practised effort of putting the flat back into the shape of a student's room before anyone else was awake to see it otherwise.

*

"So that's it," Ashwin said, back at the dhaba table, the tea gone properly cold now. "Case closed. Case study nobody reads. Back to being nobody."

"Not nobody." Satish finally looked at him properly, the first time all evening. "Just not this, anymore. There's a difference, if you let there be one."

Ashwin didn't answer that directly. He had one more question, and it had been sitting in him since before he'd even sat down, and he chose, for tonight, to let it wait a little longer than that.
