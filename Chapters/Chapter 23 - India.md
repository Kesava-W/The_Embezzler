# Chapter Twenty-Three — India

"You want the frame-up," Ashwin said. "Properly, start to finish, not the version that ended up in a Mauritian charge sheet."

Satish said nothing beyond a small tilt of his head, tea long finished, content this time to let the whole of it come from the other side of the table.

"Aadhunik wasn't chosen at random. He was chosen because he'd already spent a decade building his own motive for me." Ashwin said it the way he might have explained a piece of code, no heat in it, just structure. "Illegal shipments through his own company for years. A government he'd humiliated publicly more than once - slapped an official who came to warn him, sabotaged a flyover inauguration out of pure spite, let his own people get caught moving ganja through his harbour without so much as a change of expression. Every one of those was real. I didn't invent a single piece of it. I just needed a man the Mauritius government already half-believed was capable of anything, so that when the evidence for something he genuinely hadn't done arrived, nobody would need much convincing to close the gap themselves."

"And the house."

"The house is the part I'm actually proud of." A small, dry note, close to the first thing resembling pleasure Ashwin had allowed himself all evening. "His grandfather built that property up from a shipping clerk's savings in the nineteen-fifties - trade money first, smuggling money once trade alone stopped being enough for what the family wanted to become. And a house built on smuggling money doesn't just have a jetty, Satish, it has a way to move things nobody upstairs is meant to see moving. There was a tunnel already there. Sealed, forgotten, half-filled with decades of silt, but there. I didn't dig anything. I found what his own grandfather had already built for exactly this kind of purpose, and I finished what seventy years of neglect hadn't quite managed to erase."

"How long did it take you to find it."

"Longer than I'd like to admit. Weeks of reading old survey filings nobody had touched in decades before I even had a reason to suspect it existed." He said it almost ruefully, the closest thing to self-criticism he'd offered all evening. "Once I had the reason, the rest was just patience."

*

The seawater found the tunnel at every high tide, which meant the only safe hours to move through it were the four either side of low water, and even then it came up past his ankles in the lowest stretch, cold and black and smelling of seventy years of things that had never seen daylight. He went in from a rock shelf below the boathouse that nobody from the main house had reason to walk to after dark, a torch held low and shielded, counting the same forty-one steps he'd counted every time before this one, the walls close enough on either side to brush his shoulders if he didn't hold himself carefully through the narrowest stretch.

The den itself sat directly beneath the floor Aadhunik crossed most mornings without ever once wondering what was underneath it - cramped, low enough that Ashwin worked most nights half-crouched, a folding table, a rig he'd assembled piece by piece over months and carried down in parts small enough to fit inside an ordinary gym bag, each piece innocuous enough on its own to survive a casual search of his belongings a dozen times over. He could hear the house above him sometimes, faint and strange through a foot and a half of old stone and newer concrete - footsteps, once a chair scraping, once, unmistakably, Aadhunik's own voice raised at someone he couldn't identify, the words themselves lost but the temper unmistakable even through that much stone. He had learned, working down there, to let that sound pass through him without reacting to it at all - to keep typing through it, keep the rig running, as though the man pacing somewhere above his head were simply weather.

*

"The final piece," Ashwin said, back at the table, "was making sure they found it."

"The location slip."

"The night of the bungalow call - that was the last major action of the whole operation, and by then the frame didn't need to stay hidden anymore, it needed to be found, cleanly enough that nobody would question how they'd found it." He turned his empty cup a quarter turn, an old habit he'd have said, if asked, he'd picked up from nobody in particular. "Every other call in this operation bounced through four countries and never once sat still. That one, I let sit still. Eleven seconds, repeating, tied to a set of coordinates that led straight back to a house with a hidden room already built beneath it and a laptop already rigged to destroy itself the moment anyone opened that hatch. I didn't need them to catch me doing anything. I needed them to catch him having a room I'd already finished using."

"You planted evidence."

"I planted a location. The room, the laptop, the tunnel - all real, all his, all sitting under his own floor whether I'd ever used it or not." Ashwin said it without flinching from the distinction, or from how thin it might sound to anyone less familiar with the year and a half of work that had gone into making it true. "I didn't fabricate his guilt, Satish. I built the one visible thread that let a police force already looking hard enough finally pull on it."

Satish let the silence hold for a moment before he spoke again, and when he did, it wasn't a challenge - closer to a note being filed away for later, the way he'd filed most of what Ashwin had told him so far.

"Thorough," he said, finally. "Even for you."

"It had to be. He was never going to be convicted of the thing he actually did wrong to me - there was no 'me' for a court to recognise in any of this. He only had one house, one history, one grudge against a government that had every reason to believe it once they had somewhere real to point." Ashwin looked down at the cold tea he still hadn't touched, turning the cup a final quarter, and let it sit still this time. "Guilty of the wrong crime is still guilty of something. I made sure the room fit exactly the shape of what he already was."
