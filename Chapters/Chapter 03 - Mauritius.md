# Chapter Three — Mauritius

Six months later, Ashwin met Mrudula.

The orientation was held on the third floor of a glass building in Ebène, in a room meant for twenty-five that had thirty-one chairs squeezed into it, because the school had over-enrolled the September intake again and nobody in administration had thought to book the larger hall until it was too late. Ebène itself did not look like anywhere Ashwin had pictured when the word Mauritius had first come up - no beach in sight, no palm trees leaning photogenically over anything, just glass and steel packed close along roads still being widened, call centres and back offices humming behind tinted windows, the kind of business district that could have been dropped in from Gurgaon or Bangalore and gone unnoticed for a week. A poster on the lift wall advertised a fun run for a bank's staff welfare fund. Somebody's coffee machine, two floors up, made itself heard through the ventilation every time it finished a cycle.

He sat two rows from the back, in the seat nearest the door, which was less a choice than a habit. Around him the cohort introduced itself in the particular shorthand of people who'd all read the same brochure - a scatter of Mauritian surnames, Indo-Mauritian mostly with a few Creole and Sino-Mauritian names threaded through, three students from India, one from Réunion who kept apologising, unprompted, for an accent nobody had commented on, a Kenyan who'd transferred in from a partner campus in Nairobi and seemed to already know, somehow, exactly how many of the thirty-one chairs were going to end up empty by the second term. The MBA itself was a University of Northampton programme, delivered here rather than there, the certificate at the end carrying a British university's name over a building in Ebène's Cybercity - which was, Ashwin had come to understand in his one week on the island, more or less how a great deal of Mauritius worked. A small country that had made a business out of hosting other countries' paperwork.

The course coordinator, a harried man named Mr Seebun who would spend the next two years mispronouncing at least a third of the names in the room, was midway through the housekeeping - timetables, the library card system, a warning about the printer on the second floor that ate the first page of every document out of what he called, without any evident irony, "principle" - when the woman two seats down leaned over.

"He's going to say 'kindly note' again," she said, not quite a whisper. "Watch."

"...so kindly note," said Mr Seebun, on cue, "that classes commence promptly at nine."

She didn't laugh, not out loud, but something in her face did, quick and private, gone almost as soon as it arrived, and Ashwin found himself smiling before he'd decided to. "You've done this before," he said. "The orientation."

"My cousin did the programme two years ago. He warned me about the printer, the 'kindly note,' all of it." She held out a hand without ceremony. "Mrudula."

"Ashwin."

"India?"

"Vizag." It came out easily, the way a rehearsed thing comes out when it's been rehearsed enough times to stop feeling rehearsed. "You're from here."

"Born here. My parents came over from Chennai before I did anything as useful as being born." She said it lightly, the kind of line that had clearly been said before, worn smooth at the edges. "Third generation confused about which country's cricket team to support."

"And which one do you support."

"Whichever one's losing less badly at the time. It's the only honest position." She said it flatly enough that it took him a second to register it as a joke, and something about the delay seemed to please her more than an immediate laugh would have.

It didn't go further than that - a shared eye-roll twenty minutes later when Mr Seebun announced that the timetable he'd just distributed was, in fact, already wrong, a brief, unremarkable exchange about which elective to take in the second term. Nothing that either of them would have called a beginning, if asked. By lunch she'd drifted to a different knot of the cohort, and Ashwin to another, and that was, as far as the day was concerned, that.

He took the bus back to Quatre Bornes in the early evening, the twenty-minute ride he was already learning to tune out, past the market stalls closing up for the day - a woman folding unsold sarong fabric back into a stack faster than seemed possible, a boy chasing down a customer who'd forgotten change - and the church spire that marked his stop better than any sign did. His flat was on the second floor of a block that had been new sometime in the last decade and was ageing the way buildings in a humid climate age - fast, and mostly around the window frames. Small. A bed, a table, a kitchenette he hadn't yet used for anything more ambitious than tea. Nothing in it announced that the person living there could afford better, which was, in its way, the point.

The parcel was waiting outside his door, propped against the frame the way the building's guard always left them, a Meridian Express label wrapped in a layer of brown paper underneath it, his father's handwriting - or what passed for his father's handwriting on the customs form, block capitals, ASHWIN c/o - across the top.

He carried it in, set it on the table, and did not open it straightaway.

When he did, it was without hurry: the tape lifted rather than torn, the brown paper folded back and set aside rather than balled up, each layer inside given a moment before the next - a tin of murukku, its lid slightly dented in transit; a jar of mango pickle sealed twice against the journey, the oil inside gone thick and dark the way it only did after weeks at sea in a container hold somewhere; a folded kurta he hadn't asked for and would never wear in this heat, smelling faintly of the cupboard it had come from; a packet of the filter coffee his mother insisted he couldn't be trusted to buy for himself abroad. He turned each item over once in his hands before setting it on the table in a neat, deliberate row, the kind of attention a parcel from home doesn't usually get from a twenty-four-year-old three weeks into a new city.

Outside, the evening call to prayer from the mosque two streets over threaded faintly through the open window, and somewhere below a moped started and didn't quite catch, tried again, caught on the third attempt and puttered off into the dark.

Ashwin sat at the table for a while, the row of small home-shaped things laid out in front of him, and didn't touch any of them again that night.
