# Chapter Twelve — India

Over the months that followed, the pattern repeated itself so many times it stopped being a story and became, instead, a kind of weather.

An education trust in Lucknow, eleven crore, its founder found three days later having apparently walked into traffic he'd crossed on foot every morning for thirty years. A river-conservation NGO outside Patna, six crore, its two directors gone quiet on every channel they'd once used to court foreign donors. A women's collective in Bhopal, four crore, no body at all this time - just a founder who resigned, cited health, and was never heard from again in any capacity anyone could trace. A legal-aid network in Kochi. A tribal-welfare trust outside Ranchi, its founder's photograph appearing once in a state-edition paper and never again. Nine crore here. Fourteen there. Three crore from an outfit so small it barely had a website, drained anyway, as though scale itself had stopped being a consideration once the pattern found its rhythm. A name in a newspaper for one cycle and never again. By the fifth month the tally had stopped being a number anyone bothered adding up in real time, because by then it had become obvious it was only going to keep climbing, NGO by NGO, warning by warning, until the country's foreign-funded unrest machinery simply ran out of NGOs still willing to answer a blocked number.

Dinesh Kaul, who ran the Nirmaan Trust out of Bengaluru, was the one who broke the pattern.

He'd read every version of the story by the time his own call came - Ravish, Aarav, the two men in Bombay, a dozen names since that had barely made the news cycle at all. He kept a folder on his own laptop, quietly, that he never showed anyone on his staff - news clippings, half of them behind paywalls he'd paid to get past, arranged by date, though he could not have said, if asked, what exactly he thought the folder was going to be useful for. He didn't argue when the blurred face on his screen delivered the same lines he'd heard were being delivered everywhere. He didn't laugh, didn't call anyone weak, didn't ask for a name or a designation to report to a lawyer.

"I understand," he said, when the caller finished. "You don't need to say it twice."

"You will cease operations funded through foreign contribution, effective immediately."

"I said I understand." A pause, and then, more to himself than to the screen: "I've watched what happens to the ones who don't."

The line went dead without further ceremony, the way it apparently always did, and Kaul sat at his desk for some time afterward doing nothing at all - not calling his lawyer, not calling his wife, just sitting with the fact of the call the way a man sits with news of a diagnosis before he's decided what to do about it.

He called a press conference two days later, unprompted by anyone, at a small hotel conference room that had clearly been booked in a hurry - the backdrop still bore the previous booking's placard, half peeled off and abandoned, and someone had had to find a Nirmaan Trust banner from a storage cupboard and iron the creases out of it that morning. Three reporters from wire services, one local channel, a handful of donors' representatives who looked more confused than reassured by what they were about to hear, murmuring among themselves in the front row until Kaul cleared his throat.

"Nirmaan Trust is voluntarily suspending all programmes funded through foreign contribution, effective today," Kaul read, from a page he'd written himself and not handed to anyone to soften first, his voice steadier than his hands, which one of the wire reporters would later mention to a colleague without quite knowing why it had stuck with her. "This is not an admission of wrongdoing. It is a decision I am taking for the safety of my staff, in light of -" he chose the next words carefully, aware of exactly how much he could say without saying too much - "recent events in this sector that I think we would all be foolish to ignore."

A reporter from PTI asked if he was referring to Ravish, to Aarav, to the deaths nobody in the sector would say out loud were connected. Kaul didn't answer that directly. "I'm referring," he said, "to the fact that I would like to still be alive next month." A second reporter tried to press him on whether he'd received a specific threat, and Kaul simply repeated the line about staff safety, word for word, the way a man repeats a sentence he's decided is the only one he's willing to be quoted saying.

It made a small story. Compliance wasn't news the way defiance was - a paragraph on an inside page, no follow-up segment, the wire photo of him at the podium cropped tight enough that the peeling placard behind him never made it into print.

Three days later, his bank balance dropped to zero anyway, in the same clean increments the others had watched their own money disappear in, and this time nobody in that account, that trust, that carefully worded press conference had been given the courtesy of a warning first.

His head of security found him that evening, in the trust's Bengaluru office, after Kaul had missed a call he never missed - a habit of his, answering within two rings no matter the hour, that the guard had come to rely on more than he'd realised until the third unanswered ring sent him up the stairs himself. The door to the back office had been forced, not picked - a detail the guard noticed immediately and would keep circling back to, later, when a police officer asked him to describe what he'd seen, because nothing about a forced door matched a man who was supposed to have died quietly, the way the others had.

"Sir, the lock -" The guard's voice, when he finally got someone from the local station on the phone, kept catching on that one detail, over and over, like it was the only part of the scene his mind would let him hold onto cleanly. "Someone came in. Someone was in this room with him. This wasn't - sir, this wasn't like the others, whatever the others were." He gave his statement twice more over the following week, to two different officers, and both times arrived, unprompted, at the same forced door, as though repeating the detail enough times might make someone finally act on it.

The post-mortem would eventually settle, officially, on a break-in gone wrong, a robbery interrupted, nothing that pointed anywhere further than the room itself. Unofficially, in the Nirmaan Trust's own security logs, in an incident report that a nervous IT consultant filed and then, weeks later, forwarded somewhere it was never meant to be traceable to him, sat a detail nobody in India ever chased down: a login attempt on the trust's banking portal, timestamped four minutes before the balance began to fall, routed through a server that bounced twice before it landed, however briefly, on an IP address registered some four thousand kilometres away, on an island most of the report's readers would have needed a map to place.

Nobody in Bengaluru followed that thread. It would take a police force on the other end of it, months later, simply receiving it - unattributed, unexplained, the first loose end an otherwise flawless operation had left anywhere for anyone to pull.

By the time the country's papers stopped running the story in any form at all, the number behind it had settled at just over six hundred crore, spread across two years and every state that had once hosted an NGO willing to take a call from abroad and not ask too many questions about where the money was actually going. The silence that followed was not the silence of a problem solved. It was the silence of an entire funding network deciding, all at once and without ever coordinating the decision, that the money was no longer worth what it had started costing them.
