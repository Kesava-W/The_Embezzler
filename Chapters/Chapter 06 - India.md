# Chapter Six — India

Aarav's villa in Assagao had cost more to fit out than most of Sarika's Grant's declared annual budget, and he had never once seen the contradiction in that as a problem worth solving.

The party had been building since sunset - a DJ flown in from Mumbai for the night, a bar staffed by two bartenders who'd worked five-star properties in Bandra before Aarav's money had made it worth their while to fly down for a weekend, champagne that had cleared customs at a price nobody at the party would have to think about, least of all Aarav, who moved through his own garden that night the way a man moves through a room he owns outright - loudly, expansively, an arm around whichever shoulder was nearest, telling the same three stories about the same three deals to whoever hadn't already heard them. Fairy lights strung through the frangipani. The pool lit from underneath, turning the water the colour of the champagne. A caterer had built an ice sculpture of the villa's own gate, of all things, which was melting faster than anyone had accounted for and dripping steadily onto a cheese board nobody seemed to mind getting wet. Somewhere past midnight someone would jump into it fully clothed and Aarav would laugh harder at that than at anything said all evening.

He had heard about Ravish by then - everyone in that circle had, a whisper passed between people who'd known the man only well enough to gossip about him usefully. Aarav's read on it, offered freely to anyone who asked and several who hadn't, was that Ravish had always been soft under the confidence, the kind of man who folded the moment real pressure found him. "Weak fellow," Aarav said, more than once that night, with the specific satisfaction of a man measuring himself against a corpse and coming out ahead. It did not occur to him, not for a second, that the measurement might run the other way. One guest, an older man from an environmental trust who'd known both of them for years, listened to Aarav say it a second time and quietly excused himself toward the bar rather than agree or argue, the particular non-committal exit of a man who'd already decided, privately, exactly how frightened he himself intended to be about all of this once he got home.

The call came a little after eleven, while he still had a glass in each hand and an audience of four around him.

He took it there, in front of them, more performance than caution - held the tablet up so the others could see the blurred face on the screen too, already laughing before the voice had finished its first sentence, because to Aarav this was theatre, a story to tell at the next party rather than a threat to be taken anywhere near seriously.

"Mr Aarav. Sarika's Grant's foreign contributions are under review. You are advised to suspend all activities and cooperate, effective immediately."

"You people called Ravish also, no? I heard." He turned the tablet slightly, playing to the small crowd now, delighted with himself. "Difference is, Ravish was scared of his own shadow. Ask anyone. Me?" He spread his arms, champagne sloshing over one hand, unbothered. "I have survived two ED raids, one CBI notice, and my own chartered accountant trying to blackmail me. You think a blurred face is going to do what all of them couldn't?"

"This is your only warning."

"Warning noted." He was already turning away, tablet still in hand, playing it for the laugh it got him. "Anything else, or can I get back to my party -"

The call ended itself. He didn't notice for nearly a minute, still delivering the last line of the joke to an audience that had already started drifting back toward the music, one of them already halfway to a new conversation before Aarav had finished the punchline he'd been so pleased with.

It was Rohan - an old university friend, three drinks in, stepping away from the noise to take a call from a number he didn't recognise - who found him again eight minutes later, face gone the wrong colour for a party.

"Aarav. Aarav, listen - it's your house. Your Bombay house, the garage - your driver's on the phone, he's saying men came in, four or five of them, they've hit the security guard, hit Sanjay too -" Rohan's voice was climbing, the words arriving faster than he could organise them, his free hand pressed flat against his own chest as though that might slow either his breathing or Aarav's understanding down to a speed he could manage. "They found the room. The one behind the shelving. They're burning it, Aarav, they poured something on it and they're burning it right there in the garage - he says he can smell it from the gate -"

Twenty-five crore, in cash, that existed on no ledger anywhere - three years of skimmed grant money that had never touched a bank account precisely so it would never have to answer to one, converted to notes and locked behind a false wall for exactly the kind of day Aarav had never imagined arriving. It was gone within minutes, and unlike the number that would fall on a screen later that night, this part nobody would ever be able to prove had existed at all.

Aarav didn't laugh again after that.

He was already moving toward the house when the second call found him - not a call this time, just the NGO's banking app, open on his phone from habit, the balance sliding downward in front of him the way Ravish's had, five crore dropping in increments too clean to be anything but deliberate, and by the time it hit zero the last of the party's noise had gone strange and distant around him, a sound happening to someone else's evening.

He didn't scream the way Ravish had. What came out of him was quieter and worse - a single word, his driver's name, said to no one, into a garden full of people who had no idea yet that anything had changed. The DJ, thirty feet away, took the sudden quiet from that corner of the garden as a cue to bring the bass back up rather than as anything worth investigating.

Nobody at the party could say, afterward, exactly when Aarav had left it. Sometime past one, most agreed, though nobody had watched him go. The DJ played until nearly four, unaware for most of that time that the man whose money had paid for the night was no longer anywhere in the villa. The environmental-trust guest, one of the very few who noticed Aarav had gone quiet before he disappeared entirely, left not long after without saying goodbye to anyone, and would tell his wife on the drive home, unprompted, that something about the last hour of that party had made him want to be somewhere else.

He was found the next morning on the sand below Anjuna, past the rocks where the current pulled hardest, by a fisherman heading out before the tourists were awake. The police, when they came, took one look at the empty bottles further up the beach and the state of the tide the night before and reached the conclusion Goa's beaches reached with grim regularity most seasons - a drunk man, alone, too close to water that did not forgive carelessness. Rip current, the report would say. Alcohol a contributing factor. No sign of struggle they thought worth recording, no question they thought worth asking twice.

Nobody connected a burned room in a Bombay garage to a body on a Goa shoreline four hundred kilometres away. There was, on paper, nothing to connect.
