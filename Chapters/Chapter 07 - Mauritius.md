# Chapter Seven — Mauritius

It became a Tuesday and Thursday thing, without either of them proposing it as one.

The group project - four of them, a case study on some fictional airline's fictional turnaround that Mr Seebun had assigned with his usual "kindly note" that it would count for thirty per cent - met most evenings that fortnight at a café on the Cybercity's ground floor, the kind of place that existed to feed the office towers around it and didn't mind four MBA students nursing the same pot of tea for two hours as long as they occasionally bought a second one.

"You're not even reading the case, are you," Ashwin said, on the second evening, watching Neha scroll through something that was very clearly not the case.

"I read the executive summary. That's ninety per cent of an MBA right there." She didn't look up. "Kwame. Is 'synergy' an actual strategy, or just a word people use when they don't have one."

"Depends who's paying me to say it." Kwame didn't look up either.

"This is why we're going to get a B," Mrudula said, not quite managing to sound as disapproving as she wanted to.

Neha usually left first, some or other reason. Kwame stayed till the café itself started stacking chairs - the same café staff, a young man with an accent Ashwin still couldn't place and a habit of leaving the last two pieces of whatever biscuit came with the tea rather than clearing them, as if he'd decided, unilaterally, that the table deserved to finish in its own time. More often than either of them had planned for, it ended up being just Ashwin and Mrudula at the table, laptops open, the actual case study forgotten somewhere around the second hour in favour of everything that wasn't it.

She noticed things about him in the gaps between the work. That he was quick - too quick, once or twice, for someone who claimed to have struggled through the statistics module - fixing a broken formula in Neha's spreadsheet in the time it took the rest of them to identify which cell was wrong. That he deflected, lightly and without seeming to, whenever a question drifted too close to home - not evasive exactly, just skilled at answering a slightly different question than the one asked, and skilled enough that most people wouldn't have caught the substitution. On the Thursday of the second week she'd asked him, half-joking, whether he had any siblings, and it had taken her most of the walk to the car park to realise he'd answered a question about whether he got along with his parents instead, smoothly enough that she'd nodded along in the moment without noticing the swap. She caught it later that night, replaying the exchange the way she sometimes did with conversations that snagged on something. She didn't ask again.

That was, she was starting to understand about herself, mostly how she handled things she wanted answers to. She let them sit.

He walked her to the car park most evenings after, the short distance from the café to where her driver waited with the engine running - her parents were particular about that, a driver rather than a bus for their daughter, no matter how many times she pointed out that half her cohort managed the 57 to Quatre Bornes without incident. It was four minutes, five at most, down a paved walkway lit by the Cybercity's uniform street lamps, and neither of them ever found a way to make it take longer, though she caught herself, more than once, matching her pace to something slower than either of them needed.

"You don't have to walk me every time," she said once, not quite looking at him.

"I know."

"So why do you."

He considered it a moment, the kind of pause that in anyone else she'd have read as stalling. "Because the alternative is going straight back to my flat," he said. "This is objectively better."

It wasn't really an answer - or it was exactly an answer, dressed up as a joke so neither of them would have to do anything about it being true. She let it go the way she let most things go with him.

Up in Floréal that evening it had started to drizzle by the time the car turned off the motorway, the way it did most evenings on the plateau even when the coast forty minutes away hadn't seen a cloud all day - Curepipe's rain, her mother called it, with the particular resignation of someone who'd lived under it her whole life. Mrudula watched it streak sideways across the window and said nothing to the driver, who knew better than to make conversation on this stretch of road anyway, the wipers ticking a rhythm she'd long since stopped hearing as anything other than background.

She thought about the spreadsheet. About the way Ashwin had said "Delhi" - no, Vizag, she corrected herself, she still sometimes mixed it up, which was its own small annoyance, that she should already know a detail like that well enough not to need correcting - about the way he'd said it a beat too smoothly, the first day, like a man reading a line rather than answering a question. She thought about the four minutes in the car park, how she'd started counting them without deciding to. She thought, too, of something her mother had said the previous weekend, apropos of nothing, over breakfast - that it was a shame Mrudula never brought any of her cohort home, that a house this size should have more young people in it - and how she'd changed the subject faster than the comment strictly required, and how her mother had let her.

None of it added up to anything she could have said out loud to anyone, least of all to him. It wasn't suspicion, not really - she didn't think, sitting in the back of her father's car with the plateau rain running down the glass, that there was anything sinister underneath the smoothness. People held things back for ordinary reasons. Grief, sometimes. Habit, more often. She held things back herself, constantly, and had never once considered it evidence of anything worth investigating.

But it meant she was paying attention to him in the specific, cataloguing way a person pays attention to someone they're trying not to think about too much - the exact wording of a sentence, the four minutes, the small crooked half-smile he did when Mr Seebun said "kindly note" and caught Mrudula's eye across the table to see if she'd clocked it too. She had. Every time.

The house came up on the left, gate already swinging open for the car, and Mrudula sat with her bag on her lap for a second longer than she needed to before reaching for the door handle.

She was not going to tell him. Not tonight, not soon - possibly not for a long time yet, because telling him would make it a fact in the world rather than a fact only she was carrying, and she wasn't ready for the difference between those two things. But sitting there, in the last few seconds before the driver came round to open her door and the evening became her parents' evening again, table settings and small talk and whatever her mother wanted to discuss about the Sharma wedding, Mrudula let herself have the sentence, once, silently, the way she let herself have very little else without immediately arguing herself out of it.

*This is real,* she told herself. Just once. Just for the length of the driveway.

This had stopped being nothing.
