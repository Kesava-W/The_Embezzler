# Chapter Fifteen — Mauritius

Two weeks of silence had done more to Mrudula than the beating itself seemed to have done to Ashwin, at least from the outside - his bruises were fading on schedule; whatever was happening to her didn't show up on any timeline a doctor could have given. She'd gone through the motions of those two weeks the way a person goes through the motions of anything they've decided not to examine too closely - classes attended and not remembered, meals eaten without tasting, a version of herself performed for her parents at dinner each night that fooled at least one of them entirely.

She'd told her parents she was meeting Neha for coffee. It wasn't entirely a lie - she had, for the twenty minutes it took to explain, with as much composure as she could still manufacture on demand, that she needed the afternoon and would owe Neha a favour for it. Neha hadn't asked what the favour was actually covering, which Mrudula had been grateful for in a way she didn't have the words to explain even to herself. The taxi from Floréal to Quatre Bornes had felt longer than the flight home from her month away, and she'd spent most of it with a small paper bag on her lap, packed the night before with the particular care of someone trying not to think too hard about what she was doing while she did it - the watch wrapped in tissue paper it didn't strictly need, the scarf folded and refolded twice before she'd let herself stop.

Ashwin opened the door before she'd finished knocking a second time.

Neither of them said anything for a moment. He looked at the bag in her hands - the watch he'd given her for her birthday, a scarf, a folded card - and something in his face closed over, quietly, the way a person's face closes when they've already rehearsed how a scene like this one is supposed to go.

"Can we talk," she said. "Inside. Not on the step."

He stepped back to let her in, and closed the door behind her with a care that felt, to both of them, like it was doing more work than a door usually did.

She set the bag down on the table without a word. For a moment neither of them moved, the small distance of the flat between them feeling, to both of them, considerably larger than it was. Then she turned, and slapped him - not hard, nothing like the single sharp crack from two weeks earlier, just a soft, open-palmed contact that landed and lifted and landed again, three times, four, each one accompanied by nothing except the sound of her own breath starting to come apart.

"You said -" Slap. "- reconsider -" Slap. "- our -" Her voice cracked entirely on the last word and the last slap arrived at the same moment, gentler than any of the others, more collapse than blow. "- relationship."

Then she was against him, both arms locked around his back, crying into his shoulder the way she hadn't let herself cry once in the two weeks since, and he held her without trying to explain any of it, because there was nothing in that moment a sentence could have done that the silence wasn't already doing better. He held her long enough that his own arms started to ache with it, and didn't loosen them once.

"I brought your things back," she said eventually, muffled, not moving from where she stood. "I thought - I don't know what I thought. That if I gave them back it would feel like I'd actually ended it. It doesn't. It just feels like I'm standing in your flat holding a watch."

"Mrudula -"

"I know why you said it." She pulled back far enough to look at him, eyes red, voice steadier now than it had any right to be. "You think if we stop, Aadhunik has no reason to come after you again. You think you're protecting me by giving me an easy way to let him win."

"I can't fight him. Not the way he needs to be fought." He said it plainly, no performance in it, which somehow made it worse to hear. "He has money I can't match and a government half-afraid of him and half-owned by him, and I have a rented flat and an MBA I'm not even using. Whatever this is between us, it isn't strong enough to survive that. I don't think anything is."

"Then don't fight him here."

He went still. "What?"

"Come with me. Leave. Not - not hide somewhere on this island, I mean actually leave, get on a plane, go somewhere he has no money and no government half-owned by him and no reason to ever think of either of us again." She said it fast, like she'd been holding the sentence ready for longer than the walk from the door had given her time to plan it, the words arriving almost faster than her own breath could keep pace with. "I'll come with you. I don't care where."

Whatever he'd braced himself for on the walk to answer the door, it hadn't been this. It took him a visible second to find anything to say at all, his mouth half-opening on a sentence he abandoned before it started.

"You'd leave Mauritius. Your family. Everything you've ever known here."

"I've been thinking about it since the night he beat you nearly to death in a room I stood outside crying in while my own father slept two doors down and never once woke up." Her jaw was set now, the fear underneath it not gone but no longer running the sentence. "I'm not asking you to fix anything, Ashwin. I'm asking you to let me choose you properly, somewhere he can't undo it."

"You've thought about where, even."

"I checked my passport two days ago. It's got four years left on it." She said it almost as an aside, like it was the least significant part of what she'd just told him, though it was the detail that seemed to cost her something to admit out loud - proof, even to herself, that the thinking hadn't started tonight. "I wasn't going to tell you that part. It felt like admitting I'd already half-decided before I ever said anything to you."

"If you do this -" he started, and had to stop, and start again, because the next part mattered too much to say carelessly. "If you leave with me, you understand what that actually means. Not a holiday. Not something you can quietly walk back from in six months if it gets hard. Your parents, this house, this whole life - once you're on that plane, Mauritius doesn't forgive that easily, not for a family like yours. You would not be able to come back. Not the way you'd want to. Maybe not at all."

"I know."

"I don't think you do. Not yet. Think about it properly - not tonight, not with your face still wet from crying in my flat. Think about it when you're calm, when nobody's just beaten anyone in front of you, and if the answer is still yes after that, then we talk about what leaving actually looks like."

She looked at him for a long moment, something steadying behind her eyes that hadn't been there when she'd walked in with a bag full of returned gifts. Outside, a neighbour's radio carried faintly through the wall, some ordinary song for an ordinary evening that had nothing to do with either of them, and for a strange half-second the sound of it seemed to belong to a different, easier world than the one currently sitting between them.

"Okay," she said. "I'll think about it properly."

She didn't sound, when she said it, like a woman who expected to change her mind. She picked the small paper bag back up off the table on her way out - a detail neither of them commented on, and neither of them needed to.
