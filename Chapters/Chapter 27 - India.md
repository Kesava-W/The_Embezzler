# Chapter Twenty-Seven — India

Mrudula woke to the sound of a cupboard being opened too carefully, the particular quiet of a husband trying not to wake his wife and doing it just loudly enough to wake her anyway.

"You're up early," she said, into the pillow, not yet opening her eyes.

"Standup's at nine. I told you about this three times this week and you keep forgetting on purpose." Ashwin was at the mirror, collar half-turned, laptop bag already by the door the way it sat every weekday morning now, the most ordinary object in a life that had, as far as she was concerned, never once been anything other than ordinary. "There's coffee. Don't let it go cold arguing with me about the coffee."

She sat up anyway, watched him find his shoes, watched him check his phone twice in the particular way of a man mentally rehearsing a meeting he hadn't fully woken up for yet. It struck her, in the unremarkable way small true things sometimes strike a person before breakfast, that she still didn't entirely know how a man who'd taken an MBA to "get a break from coding" had ended up back in front of a screen within eight months of the wedding, code review comments open in one tab and, more often than she properly registered, something that looked considerably more complicated than code review in another.

He was at the door, keys in hand, when she caught his wrist.

"You know," she said, half a laugh already in it, "you know a lot than you show."

"Is that so."

"Mm." She wasn't building toward anything with it - the thought had simply arrived, the way small thoughts about a husband of eight months sometimes do, said out loud because saying it out loud was easier than deciding whether it meant anything, and forgotten, she'd have told you with complete honesty, by the time the sentence finished leaving her mouth. "Go. You'll be late for your very important meeting about other people's bugs."

She kissed him instead of waiting for an answer, quick and warm, the kind of kiss that had stopped needing an occasion months ago.

"Come home early tonight," she said, against his collar. "I don't care why. Just come home early."

"I will."

He was gone before the door had finished swinging shut behind him - down the stairs, into a morning that had nothing unusual in it at all, an ordinary man going to an ordinary job in an ordinary city, carrying, the way he had every morning since the wedding and would carry every morning after this one, the only thing about himself he had never once put down.

Mrudula didn't move for the coffee straightaway. She went instead to the drawer of the bedside table and took out the small grey scan she'd kept folded there since Tuesday - grainy, half-illegible to anyone who hadn't been shown, twice now, exactly where to look - and sat on the edge of the bed with it for a minute she hadn't planned to give it, smiling at a shape that still didn't look, to her own eye, like very much of anything at all.

The knock came a little after nine, light, once, gone before she'd crossed the room to answer it - a small box on the step, MERIDIAN EXPRESS printed across a label addressed to her this time, an order she'd placed and cancelled and placed again three times before finally letting it go through. She carried it in without opening it, set it on the shelf in the hall cupboard where he wouldn't think to look before evening, and didn't examine, even to herself, why the sight of it standing there - small, ordinary, entirely hers - had done something to her chest that the rest of the morning hadn't managed.

Mrudula drank her coffee before it went cold, and didn't think about the sentence again.

---

He came home late enough that she'd started listening for the lift before she'd admit to herself she was listening for it - half past eight, tie already loosened by the time he was through the door, the particular tiredness of a man who'd spent nine hours fixing something that hadn't been broken until someone above him decided it was.

"Long day?"

"Three people decided the sprint needed 'realigning' at four o'clock." He dropped onto the sofa, eyes half-closing the way they did on nights like this. "Don't ask. Tell me something that isn't work."

She didn't answer. She went to the hall cupboard instead, and came back with the small box, MERIDIAN EXPRESS still printed across the label, and set it on his lap without a word.

He opened one eye. "You ordered yourself something and I'm meant to be excited about it?"

"Open it."

Something in her voice, or in the way she was standing rather than sitting down beside him, made him sit up properly before he'd even got the tape off - the same old instinct that had once fixed a broken spreadsheet formula before anyone else at the table had spotted the error, reading a room correctly half a second before he had any reason given to him yet.

Inside: something small, white, absurdly soft, a single sleeve barely long enough for an adult's finger.

He went very still.

"Mrudula -"

"Tuesday," she said. "I've known since Tuesday. I couldn't decide what telling you properly was supposed to look like, so." She nodded at the box. "That, I suppose."

For a second he didn't move at all. Then whatever had been holding him upright all evening simply let go, and he was off the sofa in one motion, and she was off the ground a half-second after that, laughing before she'd decided to let herself, his arms locked round her while he spun once, twice, the little box forgotten on the floor where it had tipped from his lap.

"Ashwin - Ashwin, put me down, I'm going to be sick -"

"Not yet."

"Ashwin."

He put her down, slowly, like his arms hadn't fully agreed to the decision, and didn't let go of her even then - just stood there, forehead against hers, both of them slightly out of breath for reasons that had stopped being about the spinning.

"You're sure," he said, quieter now.

"I'm sure."

He kissed her - unhurried, the kind that had long since stopped needing an occasion and had, for once tonight, found one anyway. When it ended he didn't step back, just held her there in the middle of an ordinary Tuesday-turned-evening, one hand flat against her back like it needed the proof of her actually being under it.

"I came home early," he said eventually, into her hair, "for nothing you ever told me to expect."

"I told you to come home early. I never said why."

"No," he agreed. "You didn't."

They drifted to the sofa eventually, her feet in his lap, the little box still lying upside down on the floor where neither of them had got round to righting it. The flat had gone quiet in the way a place does once it's finally run out of things to be busy about for one evening.

She shifted against him, quiet for a moment, then said, "Do you remember the promenade. That evening, about a month before I left for the December holidays."

"Which part of it."

"You know which part."

He did.

*

The seawall was still warm from the day's sun long after the sun itself had gone, the tide out far enough that the last of the fishing boats were only just easing back in against a sky doing something extravagant with orange that neither of them was paying proper attention to. Mrudula sat on the wall with her sandals kicked off beside her, Ashwin's head heavy and warm in her lap, one hand loose around her ankle.

"I have been wanting to ask you something," he said, eyes closed.

"What is it, Ashwin?"

"How many kids do you want?"

She felt the blush before she could stop it, grateful, for once, for the dark. "Two. Maybe."

"You are disappointing me." He didn't open his eyes. "I was expecting at least twenty."

"Twenty." She laughed despite herself. "And where exactly will you get the money to take care of twenty children. Rob a bank?"

"No." A beat, unhurried, like he'd genuinely given the question thought. "Rob an NGO."

She laughed properly at that - the kind that arrives from nowhere and doesn't need to explain itself - and swatted his shoulder, and he caught her hand before it could land a second time and simply held it there against his chest instead, neither of them in any hurry to let the evening move on to whatever came after it.

*

"I still don't know why that was the joke you reached for," she said now, into the quiet of the flat, not really a question - just remembering out loud, warm with it. "An NGO. Of everything you could have said."

He laughed, low, into her hair. "I have always had a strange sense of humour."

"You do." She tipped her head back to look at him properly. "Twenty children, though. I want it on record that you said twenty first."

"And you talked me down to three. Very reasonable of you."

"Three." She said it like she was trying the number on for the first time, deciding she didn't mind the fit of it. "We'll see."

Neither of them said anything else for a while after that. The little box stayed where it had fallen, upside down on the floor. Outside, an ordinary evening carried on without them, unremarkable in every direction - and for once, in that flat, at that hour, nothing in the room was standing in for anything else at all.
