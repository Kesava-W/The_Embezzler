# Chapter One — India

The Foreign Contribution (Regulation) Amendment Bill, 2025, was listed as Item Fourteen on the day's List of Business, which was itself the first sign that someone in the ruling party had misjudged the room. Fourteen meant afternoon. Afternoon meant a House already tired of itself - the morning's Question Hour had run long over a road contract in Odisha nobody outside that state cared about, the galleries were thinning the way they always did after lunch, and the air conditioning in the central hall was fighting a losing battle against a crowd of people who had all, without exception, worn their heaviest jackets for the cameras rather than the weather.

The Minister of Home Affairs did not dress it up. He read the statement of objects in the flat voice reserved for things that are expected to pass without a fight - stricter registration for NGOs receiving funds from abroad, tighter audit windows, a new clause letting the Ministry freeze an account on suspicion alone, no court order required, not yet, not at this stage. He did not raise his eyes from the paper more than twice. He used the word *national interest* twice, evenly spaced, the way a man uses a phrase he has already decided will need to carry more weight in tomorrow's headlines than it currently carries in the room, and sat down.

For nine seconds, nothing happened.

Then the Opposition benches came up as one body, papers in the air, and the nine seconds were the last quiet the House would see that day.

"Draconian." "Witch hunt." "Which NGOs, name them, name a single one -"

The Speaker's appeals for order went unheard under it - please take your seats, please, let the House function - then unheard again, then simply repeated on a loop nobody in the well was listening to. Printed copies of the bill came out from somewhere and were torn along the spine, in twos and threes, the pieces flung toward the well rather than at anyone in particular, so that by the time the first ten minutes were through, the carpet in front of the Speaker's chair had taken on the look of a floor after a wedding rather than a legislature in session. Members crossed into the well itself, which is not allowed and happens anyway on days like this one, chanting in the call-and-response the front rows had clearly rehearsed before the sitting began - one voice pitching a line, a dozen more throwing it back, the rhythm of it more organised than the words themselves ever managed to be. On the Treasury benches, palms came down on desks in flat, rhythmic support of the minister, which did nothing to the volume except add to it, competing noise answered with more noise rather than less. A junior minister, sent up mid-sitting to read an unrelated statement on drought relief, was shouted down before he'd finished his second sentence and did not attempt a third. Twice the Speaker warned that the loudest of them would be named under the rules and directed to withdraw from the House for the rest of the sitting; twice it changed nothing, because everyone in the well already knew how rarely that warning was followed through, and how well the defiance read in tomorrow's clippings either way. One member, a woman in her third term who had built her whole career on precisely this kind of footage, turned deliberately toward the press gallery halfway through her own shouting, checking, without quite looking like she was checking, that the cameras had found her.

By eleven the Speaker adjourned the House till two. By two, till four. In the corridor outside the central hall, between adjournments, small clusters of members from both sides stood drinking tea from the same urn, trading the kind of gossip that had nothing to do with the bill at all, because a legislature at war on the floor is still, underneath it, a few hundred people who see each other every working day of their lives and have long since run out of ways to stay angry at each other between sittings. The bill itself sat on the List of Business, untouched, the way a document can sit on a desk for a week looking exactly like work in progress and exactly like nothing at all.

Outside, a different noise was building.

It started at the gates - a few dozen people, then a few hundred, placards printed rather than hand-lettered, which nobody on the ground thought twice about, because who still makes placards by hand. NGO WATCHDOG, NOT GOVERNMENT WATCHDOG, one of them read. Another, simpler: OUR MONEY, OUR RIGHT. A young man near the front, not more than twenty-two by the look of him, held his sign a little higher whenever he noticed a phone camera drift his way, and let his arm drop the rest of the time. A woman with a microphone that fed back twice before someone fixed it spoke about villages that would lose their only functioning clinic if this bill passed, about vaccination drives that ran on foreign money because no state budget had ever once found the room for them, about a country that distrusted anyone trying to help it more than it distrusted the people doing the actual harm. She had good timing. She let a silence sit after "distrusted anyone trying to help it" for exactly as long as it needed to land, and the crowd, when it answered her, sounded like it meant it. The crowd liked her. The crowd grew.

By early evening it had spread two junctions down, enough that traffic police were redirecting cars around a road that was supposed to carry the evening rush, horns building into their own kind of protest a few streets over, and enough that a satellite van from a news channel had angled itself for a wide shot with the Parliament dome just visible in the frame, which is the shot every channel wanted and none of them would call staged, because it wasn't staged, not that anyone standing there could have told you with any confidence where the organic ended and the choreography began. A few bottles came out near the barricade close to nine, thrown by someone nobody in the crowd afterward could describe with any consistency. Nobody was badly hurt. It made the nine o'clock bulletins anyway, under a ticker that read GROWING UNREST, a reporter in a windbreaker raising her voice half a register above what the noise behind her actually required, and by then the story had already left the building and started running on its own.

Inside, it ran into people who read bulletins too.

The ruling party's floor managers met that night in a room three doors down from where the sitting had adjourned, too much tea going cold on the table and not enough certainty in it. There were five of them, and the youngest, a first-time MP who had been given the job of taking notes rather than opinions, would remember afterward that nobody had actually raised their voice once in the whole meeting, which had somehow made it feel worse than if they had.

"The numbers are fine," the chief whip said, more than once, as though repetition might eventually make the sentence do more work than it was doing. "We have the votes. We have always had the votes."

"The numbers were never the problem." An older man, a minister twice over in two different governments, hadn't touched his tea since it arrived. "The problem is what it looks like on a television at seven in the evening. A bill that photographs like an attack on charities and orphanages and vaccine drives does not care how many votes we have. It only cares how many people believe it before we've finished explaining it."

"So we explain it better."

"We have had all day to explain it better, and instead we got a well full of torn paper and a crowd outside that isn't going home." He said it without heat, which was somehow worse than if he'd said it angrily. "The Opposition found its footing today faster than anyone in this room expected. That is the actual news. Not the bill."

Nobody used the word *retreat*. It sat in the room anyway, unused, the way an unspoken word sometimes takes up more space than a spoken one would have. Someone, finally, used the word *timing* instead, which meant the same thing and cost considerably less to say out loud in a room where three of the five people present had ambitions beyond this particular ministry.

The amendment was not withdrawn. It was simply not called again.

It sat where Item Fourteen had left it, technically alive, practically shelved, while the session moved on to other business and the cameras moved on to other footage and the crowd outside, given nothing left to stand against, thinned back down to the few dozen who had started it, then to fewer than that, then to a handful of placards left leaning against the barricade with nobody left to hold them. Within a week the story was gone from the bulletins entirely, replaced by a cricket selection controversy that occupied roughly four times the airtime the bill itself had ever managed.

No one in the House that day would have called it a defeat. Nothing had been voted down. No minister had resigned, no clause had been struck out, no one had lost anything they could point to on paper. The government had simply, quietly, decided that this was not the week to find out how far it could push. It filed the bill the way governments file things they intend to return to - not gone, only waiting - and turned its attention to matters that could be finished by Friday.

Six hundred kilometres and several thousand crores away, nobody involved had any idea a bill with their name nowhere on it had just failed to pass.

That would take longer to fix than an afternoon in Parliament.
