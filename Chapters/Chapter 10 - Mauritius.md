# Chapter Ten — Mauritius

He missed two classes before anyone thought it was worth mentioning, and a third before Neha said it out loud in the cohort group chat - *has anyone actually heard from Ashwin* - the kind of message that sits unanswered for an hour before someone finally does something about it instead of just liking it. Kwame offered to swing by after his own lecture. Neha said she would if she didn't have a submission due. Both of those plans dissolved into the ordinary business of a Tuesday the way good intentions on a group chat usually did.

It was Mrudula who went.

She told herself, on the bus, that she was going because she happened to be free that afternoon and someone should, not because the thought of him alone and unwell in that small flat had lodged itself somewhere she couldn't easily talk it back out of. She rehearsed, somewhere around Rose Hill, the exact tone she'd use if he opened the door looking merely tired and slightly embarrassed to be fussed over - breezy, unbothered, in and out in ten minutes with a joke about him skipping class to avoid a group presentation. She kept telling herself that right up until he opened the door - barely opened it, more leaned against it - skin gone grey-pale under the heat flush, eyes not quite tracking her properly, and every version of the story she'd been telling herself on the bus fell away at once.

"Ashwin -"

"I'm fine." The word came out in a voice that made a liar of it. "Bit of a fever."

"You look like you're dying."

"...Bit of a big fever."

She was already inside before he'd finished the sentence, one arm under his to keep him upright, already deciding things he was in no state to argue with - which drawer his clean clothes were in, which of the two unwashed cups on the counter was less objectionable, whether the flat had enough clean water in it to last a night, none of which he'd have agreed to be asked about even if he'd had the strength to protest.

The clinic near Quatre Bornes took one look at the fever chart and the rash starting to bloom faintly across his forearms and didn't need long to say the word.

"Dengue, most likely. We'll confirm with a blood test, but at this time of year -" The doctor didn't finish that sentence, and didn't need to. She wrote as she talked, the pen moving faster than her voice, a woman who had clearly said some version of this sentence a dozen times already that season. "Rest. Fluids, more than he thinks he needs. Paracetamol only - nothing else, you understand? No ibuprofen, nothing that thins the blood, not while we don't know what his platelets are doing."

"And then he's fine?" Mrudula asked. "Once the fever breaks?"

"The fever breaking is not the good news you think it is." The doctor looked at her properly for the first time, taking her measure, the pen briefly still. "That's when we watch him closest. Not now, while he's burning - after. You understand? The next week is not for relaxing." She tore the prescription free and held it out, not to Ashwin, but to Mrudula, as though she'd already worked out in the space of five minutes exactly which of the two people in front of her was going to be doing the actual watching.

Mrudula understood. It was, in fact, the part that frightened her most - worse than the fever itself, that the danger wasn't now, while he burned, but after, in the days once it broke, when the body sometimes turned on itself in ways that looked, from the outside, like recovery.

She didn't go home that night. Both her parents were away that week - a wedding on her mother's side, back in Chennai, that Mrudula had begged off attending on the grounds of coursework - which left nobody to call except her mother's mobile, roaming and inconveniently timed, for a short, controlled conversation that left out most of what mattered. Her mother, distracted by a hundred things of her own that week, didn't press.

The week that followed had no single moment in it worth telling as a story on its own. A cool cloth, wrung out and refolded more times than could possibly have made a difference, laid against a forehead that stayed hot long after it should have started cooling. Meals she cooked in a kitchen not her own, learning by trial where things lived, burning the first attempt at rice badly enough that the smell lingered in the flat for a day. A night on the second day when his temperature climbed past what the thermometer wanted to display and Mrudula sat on the floor beside the bed with her phone in her hand, the clinic's number already dialled and her thumb hovering over the call button for forty minutes she would later be unable to account for in any detail, because she'd spent all forty of them simply watching him breathe. Somewhere in that same fevered night he'd muttered something she couldn't place - not her name, not anything she recognised as English or otherwise, just a clipped fragment that sounded, oddly, less like delirium than like counting, *don't lose the thread* said twice, low and urgent, before he settled again. She put it down to the fever, the way she put down everything that week, and didn't think of it again until much later, when there was finally a reason to. A morning on the fifth day when he insisted, thick-voiced and stubborn in the specific way of a man who has decided being unwell is now an insult to his dignity, that he was well enough to sit up at the table for breakfast.

"I have been in this bed for five days," he said. "I would like to eat like a person, at a table, like a person does."

"You'll sit up when the doctor says you can sit up."

"I'm not made of glass, Mrudula."

"You very much might be, right now, so humour me." She was already back at the stove, not looking at him, which was the only way she managed to sound as unmoved as she wanted to. "Two more days."

"One more day."

"Two."

He lost the argument, and made a show of losing it badly, and ate his breakfast in bed with the particular sulk of a man who has decided that dignity, on reflection, can wait a little longer than his appetite - a sulk that lasted exactly as long as the first bite of toast, after which he ate the rest without complaint and pretended he hadn't.

He sat up two days later anyway, when the doctor did say so, and by then something in the room between them had rearranged itself so completely that neither of them tried very hard to pretend it hadn't. There was less performance in it now - fewer jokes deployed to fill silences, more silences simply allowed to sit, the two of them having spent a week learning what the other one looked like at their least composed and finding that neither had flinched from it.

It was the seventh evening, fever properly broken now, colour back in a way that made Mrudula's chest loosen for the first time in a week, when he said it - not staged, not building toward anything, just said, the way a fact gets said once a person has run out of reasons not to say it.

"I love you."

He said it looking at the cup of tea in his hands rather than at her, which somehow made it land harder than if he'd looked up.

She didn't answer with words at first. She put her own cup down, careful about it even now, and crossed the small distance of the flat to where he was sitting, and put her arms around him the way she'd wanted to for most of the week without letting herself, and wept - not loudly, not for long, just the release of seven days finally finding somewhere to go - against a shoulder that had, only that morning, still felt too fragile to lean on.

"Hey." His hand found the back of her head, careful, like she was the one who'd been unwell. "I'm okay. I'm here."

"I know," she said, finally, muffled against his shoulder. Nothing more than that. It didn't need to be more than that.

Outside, on the step, unheard by either of them, the courier set a parcel down against the door frame and left the way he always did, without knocking.

Neither of them noticed it that evening. It would still be there in the morning, waiting, the way it always was.
