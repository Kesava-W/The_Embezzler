# Chapter Twenty-Six — India

The fifth meeting was shorter than the others, and Satish arrived at it looking, for the first time all week, like a man closing a file rather than opening one. Even his usual half-finished cup of tea sat further from him than before, as though tonight didn't require the prop.

"The Mauritius government has been formally informed," he said, once the tea had come and gone the way it always did. "Not publicly - through the usual channel, Ministry to Ministry, four people in that country who now know what actually happened under one of their own citizen's floors, and every one of them under an obligation that makes your operational security look casual by comparison."

"How did they take it."

"About as well as you'd expect a country to take being told a foreign intelligence service ran an eighteen-month operation on their soil, resulting in a death toll they hadn't previously connected to anything, without so much as a courtesy call beforehand." Satish said it without much heat in it, an old argument he'd clearly already had, elsewhere, on Ashwin's behalf. "There was a strongly worded communication. There was, I'm told, a considerably less strongly worded meeting the following week, once the Ministry reminded them what the relationship between our two countries has actually looked like for the last sixty years - the aid, the coast guard cooperation, the fact that half the population there can trace a grandparent back to villages most Indians couldn't find on a map. Nobody in Port Louis wanted this to become the thing that defined us to each other."

"So it's resolved."

"It's managed, which is not quite the same thing, but it will do." Satish allowed himself something close to a dry smile. "Assurances were given. Any future operation on Mauritian soil gets disclosed to their government in advance, formally, going forward - which costs us very little, since an operation like this one only works once, on a country that doesn't yet know to watch for it. Aadhunik stays exactly where he is. The official account of the bungalow massacre stays exactly what it already was. And nobody outside four rooms on two continents will ever know Rumi's Charity, Sarika's Grant, Sher's Care, or the other twenty-six names on that list had anything to do with a man from Visakhapatnam who was never, on paper, anywhere near any of it."

*

Some four thousand kilometres away, in an office that smelled faintly of old paper and sea damp, Assistant Commissioner Kevin Appadoo sat across from a superior who had called him in without explaining why, on a Sunday, which was explanation enough on its own before either of them said a word. What followed wasn't a briefing so much as a warning dressed as a courtesy - some of what closed his case, he was told, hadn't arrived the way the file said it had, and he was neither to ask further nor to repeat what he'd just been told to anyone, including the officers who'd stood beside him in that garden. No document changed hands. Nothing was written down in front of him. Appadoo said "understood" once, and meant it rather less than the word usually implied, and went home that evening and said nothing to his own wife about why he'd been called in on a day off, only that it was nothing, only paperwork, in a tone that made her decide, wisely, not to ask twice.

*

Ashwin turned his own cup, an unconscious echo of the habit he'd spent five evenings watching Satish do without ever mentioning it. "That's it, then."

"That's it." Satish stood, buttoning his jacket against a night that had turned, in the last hour, properly cold for the season. "The department will not need you again for this. If it needs you again for something else, someday, that will be its own conversation, on its own day, and you are under no obligation to say yes to it."

"And if I never hear from you again."

"Then you'll have had exactly the life you were promised when this started - the one where none of it happened, as far as anyone in it besides you is ever allowed to know." Satish paused at the edge of the dhaba's tin awning, looking back at him for a moment with something that might, in a better-lit room, have resolved into actual warmth. "Go home, Ashwin. Your wife is going to start asking questions if you keep having this many late nights."

He was gone before Ashwin could find anything worth saying back to that, the sound of a car door somewhere down the unlit lane the only sign he'd ever been there at all.
