# Chapter Eleven — Mauritius

The Ebène flyover had taken the better part of two years to build and, by the government's own reckoning, was worth making a morning of - a ribbon, a podium, a scatter of ministers standing at the correct respectful distance behind the Prime Minister, the press lined up along the barricade with cameras angled to catch the motorway opening out behind him like something out of a brochure. Chairs had been set out for dignitaries who mostly stood anyway, preferring to be seen doing so. A brass band from one of the technical colleges had been rehearsing the national anthem in the car park since six, running through it twice more than anyone actually needed. It was the kind of event that existed mostly to be photographed, which was, in its own way, exactly what made it worth hitting.

Aadhunik hadn't planned it for long. Four days, if that - a phone call to a man he trusted to find men he didn't need to trust personally, made from the same chair he took most calls from, the sea behind him doing its ordinary indifferent thing while he arranged for a road opening to go wrong.

"The flyover, Friday morning. I want it on every camera there."

"How far do you want them to go, boss?"

"I don't care how far. I care that it is ugly, and that it is on camera, and that everyone watching the news tonight understands this government cannot even open a road without someone telling them what they actually are." A pause, and then, almost as an afterthought: "Pay them well. Men who feel underpaid talk."

"Understood, boss. Six should be enough for the noise you want."

"Six is fine. I am not asking for an army. I am asking for forty seconds of footage."

He hadn't thought much beyond that. He hadn't needed to. The Ganja bust was three weeks old and still costing him licences he'd held without trouble for a decade; some correction seemed owed to somebody, even if the somebody in question would end up being six men he'd never met and a road nobody would remember the name of by next year.

It went wrong from the first minute.

Six of them came at the barricade from two directions, banners half-unfurled, the paint on them still tacky enough to smear across at least one man's own hands before he'd got two words of the slogan into the air. A scuffle at the tape - a photographer's tripod going over, somebody's placard catching a policewoman across the shoulder before she'd even properly registered what was happening, a ripple of alarm moving through the front rows of the crowd before most of them had actually seen anything worth being alarmed by. For perhaps ninety seconds the ceremony did, briefly, look like it might actually be interrupted.

Then it wasn't.

The Prime Minister's own security detail had the first two men on the ground before the ribbon had finished swinging from where someone had grabbed at it. Uniformed police, thick along a barricade line that six men with tacky paint and no plan beyond arrival had badly underestimated, closed the gap within the minute - one goon went down attempting to vault a low wall he'd clearly never actually looked at before deciding to vault it, landing badly enough that he stopped being a problem for entirely mechanical reasons; another simply stood still once he understood the numbers weren't going to work in his favour, and stopped resisting the moment the first pair of handcuffs found him. A third dropped his banner and tried, briefly and unsuccessfully, to look like a bystander. By the time the Prime Minister had been quietly, efficiently walked two steps back from the tape - more out of protocol than any real danger, the whole thing already over by the time most of the crowd understood it had started - all six were face down or cuffed or both, and the ribbon, slightly crumpled now, was cut anyway, twelve minutes later than scheduled, to a smaller and more subdued round of applause than the ceremony had originally been written for.

One of the six, being processed at the station later that morning, would tell an interviewing officer more than his employer would ever have wanted repeated - not a name, he didn't have one worth giving, but a phrase, offered up unprompted as though it might buy him something: *he told us to be paid well, said men who feel underpaid talk*. The officer wrote it down without knowing yet what it would eventually be worth, filed under a case that wouldn't connect to anything larger for weeks.

It made the evening bulletins as a footnote to the flyover's opening rather than a story in its own right - GOONS DETAIN AT FLYOVER LAUNCH, forty seconds of coverage sandwiched between the ribbon-cutting and the weather, a police spokesperson stepping up to a microphone just long enough to deliver one clean sentence. "All six men detained are known associates of Aadhunik Marine Freight. Investigations are ongoing." Then the anchor had already moved on, brightening for the forecast, as though the sentence just spoken belonged to a different, smaller bulletin entirely.

Aadhunik watched it twice.

"Sir." One of the household staff, hovering too close to the doorway, clearly unsure whether saying anything at all was the safer option. "Should we prepare a statement? Deelchand is asking -"

"Tell Deelchand to prepare nothing." Aadhunik didn't look away from the screen. "We say nothing. We give them nothing further to attach my name to."

The staff member lingered a moment longer than was wise, as though there were a follow-up question worth risking, then thought better of it and left the room without another word, pulling the door most of the way shut behind him.

He didn't see six badly organised men who'd never had a chance against a police line they hadn't bothered to study first. What he saw, replayed a second time with the volume up, was a government that had reached, without hesitation and without proof anyone had bothered presenting to him personally, straight for his name - not "unidentified men," not "a group with links under investigation," but his company, his surname, attached to a segment forty seconds long on a night that was supposed to belong to a road. They had not needed evidence. They had barely needed the men in custody to say anything at all. His name had done the rest of the work for them, the way it always did lately, the way it had been doing since long before six fools with wet paint had ever agreed to stand near that barricade.

It did not occur to him, watching the footage a third time with the sound off, the minister's mouth moving silently through the same forty seconds, that he was the one who had sent them.

What occurred to him instead - settling now into something less like anger and more like conviction, the kind that doesn't need refreshing each time because it has stopped requiring new evidence to hold - was that this was simply how it would go from here. Every move the state made would look, from where he sat, like confirmation of something he'd already decided was true. He turned the television off before the bulletin reached its second story, and did not think about the flyover again.
