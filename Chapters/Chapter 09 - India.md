# Chapter Nine — India

By the time it was Vikas and Manish's turn, they had already stopped pretending it wasn't coming.

Aarav had been a friend, not just an acquaintance - Sher's Care and Sarika's Grant had shared a stall at the same CSR fair eighteen months back, shared a chartered accountant for a while too, before Vikas got twitchy about it and insisted they split the man's fees three ways instead of pooling them, as though separate invoices could somehow separate their fates. Ravish they'd known less well, mostly through the same circuit of felicitation dinners and hotel-lobby fundraising lunches every NGO of a certain size eventually orbited into - a handshake twice a year, a WhatsApp forward on festivals. But two deaths inside a fortnight, both men who'd had money one evening and didn't have it the next, had done what nothing else in nine years of running an NGO together had managed. It had actually frightened them.

They talked about it the way frightened men talk about anything that frightens them without naming it directly - a joke about "our turn next," said once, laughed at once, never repeated, because saying it a second time would have made it true in a way the first time hadn't quite managed. Vikas wanted to go to the police. Manish pointed out, correctly, that there was nothing yet to report - no threat received, no call, nothing but a pattern only the two of them seemed to have noticed, and a pattern wasn't evidence of anything a station house would act on.

So they split the joint account's password the way men who'd watched too many films might, half each, agreed over a jittery phone call neither of them fully trusted the other's line for, both of them lowering their voices at points for no reason either could have defended if asked. The last message Manish had from Vikas, sent not long before he switched his own phone off, was almost jaunty by comparison to everything since - *relax, both of us being paranoid, will laugh about this next week* - the kind of thing a man writes to convince himself as much as the person reading it. Manish took his half, turned his phone off the way a frightened man turns off a phone - like the act itself was protection, a small ritual of control in a week that had offered him none - and checked into a hotel in Andheri East under a name that wasn't his, three floors up, curtains drawn against a city he could usually navigate with his eyes shut and now couldn't bring himself to look at from a window. He told the front desk, twice, to hold all calls. He told them a second time because the first clerk's nod hadn't seemed convinced enough.

Vikas stayed where he was. There hadn't seemed to be anywhere better to go, and some part of him - the part that still wanted, even now, to believe this was survivable through ordinary caution - reasoned that splitting up made them harder to find together, without quite following the thought to where it actually led.

The room phone rang a little after nine that night.

Manish stared at it for four full rings before he understood it wasn't a mistake, wasn't housekeeping, wasn't anything that belonged in a room he'd told the front desk, twice, to hold all calls for. He'd told them. He'd been specific about it. The phone kept ringing anyway, the sound too loud in a room that small, bouncing off a wardrobe mirror he'd angled away from the bed an hour earlier because he hadn't wanted to catch his own face in it.

He picked it up because not picking it up felt, in that moment, like the more frightening choice. *Will laugh about this next week*, he thought, absurdly, in the half-second before he had the receiver fully to his ear.

What came down the line wasn't a voice addressed to him at all - it was Vikas, mid-scream, the sound arriving raw and un-arranged the way real screaming does, nothing like the clean terror of a film. Under it, faint, other sounds Manish's mind refused to organise into anything he could later describe with any precision - a door, maybe, or something heavier. Then nothing. Not a click, not a dial tone. Just silence holding the line open, patient, as though whoever had connected the call wanted him to sit with exactly that silence for as long as it took.

Manish did not remember, afterward, how long he stood there before he put the receiver down.

He should have left it off the hook. He should have left the room, the hotel, the city if there'd been a way to manage it. What he did instead was smaller and more human than any of that - he reached for his own phone, the one he'd turned off six hours earlier out of an instinct he couldn't now locate the shape of, and turned it back on, because the only thought his body would let him finish was that he needed to call Vikas, needed to hear that the scream had been something else, anything else, and the fastest way he knew to do that was the phone in his own hand.

It took eleven seconds to power on. He watched the screen the whole time, the little wheel spinning, willing it faster, his thumb already hovering over where the dial pad would appear the instant it let him in.

The screen lit. Signal bars climbed, one, then two, then three, the phone chiming twice with messages that had queued up in the hours it had sat dark. Somewhere in that half a minute of ordinary, forgettable technology doing exactly what it was built to do, something else woke up alongside it - quietly, without asking permission, the way the rest of that week had gone for everyone whose name had come up on a list Manish would never see and could not have imagined existed.

He never got as far as dialling.

The banking app was still open from that afternoon, a habit he hadn't managed to break even in hiding, checking a balance that had frightened him more each time he looked at it. He watched it now without meaning to - a reflex, the screen already lit, his thumb already there - and saw the number begin to fall the same way he'd heard, secondhand, that Ravish's had, that Aarav's had. Not fast enough to look like an accident. Not slow enough to stop. He watched three lakh become one become nothing, in numbers that kept updating a full second after he'd already understood what he was looking at.

He didn't scream. There was, by that point, nobody left in the room to hear it if he had.

---

Two days passed with neither man reachable by anyone who tried - a niece of Vikas's, worried enough to call the police herself on the second day when nobody answered a phone that usually rang through within three rings, and worried enough, by the third unanswered call, to drive to his residence herself rather than wait for a constable who kept saying "give it some more time, madam." What she found, and what the hotel's housekeeping staff found within the same few hours on the other side of the city, would end up in two separate reports that no one, for a long while, thought to read side by side.

Vikas, at his own residence, in a chair he hadn't left since whatever had happened to make him scream the way Manish had heard him scream - a chair angled, oddly, toward the door rather than away from it, as though the last thing he'd done was watch it. Manish, in the Andheri hotel room, curtains still drawn, the door still bolted from the inside the way he'd left it two nights before, the housekeeping trolley left untouched in the corridor outside for two full days because a "Do Not Disturb" card had been slotted into the handle and nobody had thought to question it until the smell did the questioning for them.

Between them, two small bottles, both empty, both a poison ordinary enough to buy at any pesticide shop in the city without a licence or a question asked. The post-mortem would call it consistent with self-administration. It would not, and could not, explain why two men four kilometres apart had apparently reached for the same bottle, in the same week, within hours of each other - a detail the report noted and did not pursue, because there was, on the surface of it, nothing illegal about two frightened men choosing to die the same way.
