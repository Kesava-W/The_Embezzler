# Chapter Nineteen — Mauritius

The warrant came through a little before five in the morning, signed by a magistrate roused out of bed for it, on the strength of a single anomaly Reshma Bunwaree had found buried in three days of call-routing data nobody outside her unit had thought worth a second pass. She'd been at it since midnight, the office empty except for her and a cold cup of tea she kept forgetting to finish, the trace map open across two monitors long after everyone else on the floor had gone home.

The bungalow in Rajokri had made it onto Interpol's wire within a day of the bodies being found - sixty dead in a country that didn't share a border with Mauritius, the kind of case that got flagged and forwarded rather than filed and forgotten, and forwarded, eventually, straight to the desk of the one Mauritius unit that had already been quietly chasing a version of this exact signature for months. The call that had set the goons on the NGO heads had bounced the way every call in this pattern bounced - four countries, proxy after proxy, a location that refused to sit still.

Except once, near the very end of it, for eleven seconds it had.

"It's not clever," Bunwaree told Appadoo, turning her screen so he could see the trace laid out plain, her voice carrying the particular flatness of someone who has been awake far longer than is good for her judgement and knows it. "That's what took me so long to see it - I kept looking for something sophisticated and missing that this is the opposite. Every other ping on this call bounces clean. This one blinks. On, off, on, off, always the same coordinates when it's on." She tapped the address on the map, a stretch of coastline neither of them needed the pin to recognise. "Somebody wanted this address found. Just not easily, and not by anyone who wasn't already looking hard enough to earn it."

Appadoo didn't ask who. He already knew the shape of the answer well enough not to need it spelled out - only that whoever had built this had, for reasons that made his stomach turn slightly to consider, apparently decided it was time to hand Aadhunik over. He read the printout twice anyway, as though a second reading might yield a different name, and then reached for his phone to wake the magistrate.

They went in at first light, eleven officers, Bunwaree's forensic technician trailing behind the entry team with a case of equipment nobody expected to need until the ordinary parts of the search had already failed. The estate gate stood open before they'd finished announcing themselves, a guard on duty too surprised by the number of vehicles to consider doing anything but stepping aside. Aadhunik met them at his own front door in a dressing gown, more affronted than afraid, already reaching for the word *lawyer* before anyone had finished reading him the warrant.

"This is harassment. I will have every one of you named in a complaint by lunchtime -"

"You're welcome to file it," Appadoo said, "after the search."

He said it evenly, without theatre, and if there was any particular weight in his voice as he said it, he kept it small enough that nobody watching could have called it anything but professional.

The house gave up nothing for the first two hours. Room after room, staff standing in doorways with the specific stillness of people who have decided that stillness is the safest posture available to them, and nothing anywhere that matched the coordinates Bunwaree's trace had pinned to this address with such stubborn insistence. Wardrobes opened and closed. A safe in the study, emptied and found to hold nothing more interesting than deeds and a stack of foreign currency. It was the garden that finally gave it away, and not through anyone's diligence. The household's dog - an ageing, unremarkable mixed breed nobody had thought to lock away before the search began - wouldn't leave a section of the lawn near the old boathouse alone, scratching at the grass with an insistence one of the younger officers found more amusing than significant until he actually looked at the patch the animal wouldn't quit. The grass there sat a half-shade greener than the rest, too recently disturbed to have properly taken. Beneath it, once the digging started, a hatch that had never appeared on a single plan Aadhunik had ever filed with the council.

The room below was small, cramped, humid with sea air that had no business being that far under a garden. A folding chair. A router, still blinking uselessly at nothing. And a laptop, its casing cracked open along one seam, its insides fused into something the forensic technician would later describe, in a report nobody outside the unit would ever fully understand, as consistent with a deliberate thermal event rather than accidental damage - a machine that had known, the instant the hatch above it opened, exactly what it was supposed to do about that.

"Whoever was down here," the technician said, crouched by the wreckage, torch angled into the melted casing, "knew we were coming before we did."

Appadoo stood at the top of the hatch for a moment before climbing back out, looking down at a hidden room that had, for reasons still well outside anyone in this garden's understanding, been left for them to find rather than erased along with everything else. He thought, briefly and without needing to dwell on it, of a government car and a slapped face two years and a great deal of patience ago - the sting of it fresher in memory than it had any business being after this long. He straightened his collar - an old habit, the same small, deliberate correction he'd made walking off that verandah the first time - and said nothing about it to anyone.

Aadhunik was arrested within the hour, the charge sheet growing by the day after that as investigators worked backward from the room beneath his garden to everything it could plausibly be tied to - the NGO embezzlement, the Rajokri deaths by extension, a case built less on any confession than on the simple, mounting impossibility of the room having belonged to anyone else. He would spend the better part of the next year in remand before a trial date was even set, his lawyers filing motion after motion against evidence that never quite gave them anywhere useful to push. None of it would end the way any version of Aadhunik had ever imagined his own story ending.

By the time the file finally closed, months later, it would carry Bunwaree's name on the technical annex and Appadoo's on the arrest report, and neither of them, in the small satisfaction of finishing something that had genuinely earned its difficulty, would spend much time wondering why the trail had been so much easier to follow, right at the very end, than it had any right to be.
