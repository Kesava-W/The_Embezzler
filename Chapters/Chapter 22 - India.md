# Chapter Twenty-Two — India

"The parcels," Ashwin said, the next time they met - a different dhaba this time, further out, same tin roof, same tea nobody drank, the same tea-stall boy's counterpart here setting down two cups with the identical practised indifference. "That's what I actually want to know. Not the accounts, not the frame-up. How did instructions reach me for eighteen months without a single one of them ever getting traced back to anyone."

"Because nobody was ever looking at a courier label for a spy novel's worth of tradecraft." Satish said it plainly, without any of the pride a lesser man might have let creep into an answer this good. "They were looking, if they were looking at all, for a signal. A phone call, a data packet, something that behaved like information. Nobody flags a parcel of pickle and murukku from a mother to her son as a security concern. That was the whole design."

"The parcels were real. Meridian Express, the customs forms, all of it."

"The company's real. The delivery is real. What isn't real is where they originated." Satish turned his cup a quarter turn on the table, an old habit Ashwin had noticed and never once asked about, the same small rotation he'd made at the last dhaba and would probably make at the next. "Every parcel you received in Mauritius was assembled and dispatched by the department. Not your mother. Not your father. The 'from home' label, the handwriting on the customs form - forged, competently, by someone whose entire job is making a stranger's handwriting look like a father's. Your parents sent you exactly nothing during those eighteen months, and have no idea, to this day, that anyone ever needed them to."

Ashwin sat with that for a moment, the tea in front of him going cold the way it had at every one of these meetings so far.

"They never knew."

"They never knew. It was safer for them that way, and safer for the operation. A parent who doesn't know he's part of something can't be pressured into confirming it, can't slip, can't be watched by anyone smart enough to watch the family instead of the son." Satish's voice didn't soften delivering it, but it didn't need to. "You can be angry about that if you want. Most people are, when they hear it."

"I'm not angry." Ashwin turned it over once more before he said the rest. "I'm trying to work out whether I should be surprised. I don't think I am."

*

The tin of murukku sat on the table exactly the way it always did, first item out of the parcel, set down in its row ahead of the pickle jar and the folded kurta he'd never once worn - the same order every time, a small domestic ritual he'd never once had reason to examine.

He picked through it the way anyone might - a piece to the mouth, unremarkable, the taste of home doing whatever small work it did for a man a year and a half into a cover he'd stopped thinking of as a cover most days. But his fingers were counting as they went, not eating, weighing each spiral against a memory of what the others should feel like, the difference so slight that anyone watching him would have seen nothing but a man enjoying a snack, until they found the one that sat a fraction heavier than it had any right to. He set that one aside from the rest without ceremony, the way a man sets aside a piece he simply isn't in the mood for, and kept eating the others as though nothing about the evening had just changed.

Later, alone, the flat quiet around him and the rest of the parcel put away, he broke that one piece open over the sink, the tap running softly the whole time, a habit he'd built specifically so the sound of running water would be the only thing anyone outside the door could have heard.

Inside, rolled tight enough to have survived a week in transit without a crease showing on the outside, was a strip of paper no wider than his little finger - a name, an account number, a location in Mauritius he'd need to be near within the week, six lines of instruction compressed into a space that would have fit inside a fortune cookie. He read it once, all the way through, the way he'd been trained to read anything he could only afford to see a single time, his eyes moving down the strip without pause, without going back to check anything. Then he held a match to one corner and watched the paper curl and blacken and go, ash falling into the sink in a small grey scatter he rinsed away without looking at it twice.

He ate the rest of the murukku standing at the counter, unhurried, the same way he ate it every time, because there was no version of that evening in which anyone watching him - and someone, somewhere, might always have been watching him - would have seen anything on his face beyond a man enjoying a snack from home.

There had been one evening, months into the routine, when he hadn't heard her key in the door quickly enough - the strip of paper still open flat on the counter, his thumb halfway to the match, when her voice had called his name from the hallway a full three seconds before she actually appeared. He'd folded it once, closed his fist around it, and had it in his pocket and a spoon in his other hand, stirring nothing in particular, by the time she rounded the corner. She'd asked what he was making. He'd said he wasn't sure yet. It was the only time in eighteen months the margin had been that thin, and he'd never told her, and never would.

*

"That's what 'from home' meant," Satish said, back at the table, watching something settle in Ashwin's expression that he clearly recognised from other operatives, other debriefs, other men working out the same arithmetic in the same order. "Every parcel. Every single one, start to finish. There was no version of your family in any of it, except the version we built to make sure nobody ever looked twice."

"And if I'd gotten one wrong. Eaten the wrong piece, missed it, thrown the whole tin out because I wasn't hungry that week."

"Then we would have found another way to reach you, and you'd never have known the first way had failed." Satish said it without any weight at all, the flattest sentence he'd offered all evening. "You were never the only safeguard, Ashwin. You were just the one that worked."

Ashwin didn't have an answer for that, and didn't try to find one. He thought, instead, of every parcel he'd ever laid out in a neat row on a table in Mauritius, of Mrudula watching him do it once or twice without ever asking why, of eighteen months of his mother's cooking that his actual mother had never once touched - and said nothing about any of it, the way he'd been saying nothing about most things, competently, for longer than he'd let himself properly count.
