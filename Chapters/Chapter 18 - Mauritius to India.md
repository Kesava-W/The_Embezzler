# Chapter Eighteen — Mauritius to India

She left at half past one, one bag, the gate easing shut behind her by hand so it wouldn't announce itself the way it always did on the latch.

The cab was waiting exactly where he'd told her it would be, engine already running, the driver asking no questions a fare paid in cash at that hour hadn't already answered for him. Floréal fell away behind the window, dark and familiar and already, somehow, a place she was leaving rather than a place she lived. She didn't look back at the house. She'd told herself, packing that one bag two nights earlier, that she wouldn't, and she kept the promise mostly by not giving herself the chance to break it.

Ashwin was already at the terminal when she got there, and for the length of one long breath neither of them said anything at all - just stood, her bag still in her hand, his arms already opening before she'd fully closed the distance between them.

"You came," he said, into her hair.

"You sound surprised."

"A little. Good surprised."

"I've had four hundred euros in a sock drawer since October," she said, against his shoulder, almost too quietly to catch over the terminal noise, the admission arriving sideways, unprompted, like something she'd been carrying alone long enough that it needed saying to someone other than herself. "Just in case. I wasn't going to mention it unless I needed to." She didn't explain further, and he didn't ask her to - there'd be time later for the parts of this she'd apparently been building toward longer than either of them had said out loud.

The flight to Chennai left a little before dawn, a short connection onward to Visakhapatnam eating what remained of the morning, and somewhere in the blur of boarding passes and departure boards and the particular non-time of an airport transit lounge, the last of Mauritius simply stopped being anywhere either of them could see out a window. She slept for most of the second leg, her head against his shoulder, and woke to a landing announcement in a voice that wasn't hers and a heat, stepping off the plane, that felt like a different version of the one she'd left behind.

His parents were waiting at arrivals, close enough to the gate that Mrudula barely had time to register she'd landed before she was being folded into a stranger's embrace.

His mother cried before anyone had said a word of explanation. His father asked exactly one question - "You're sure?" - directed at Ashwin, who answered it by taking Mrudula's hand rather than with anything spoken, which seemed to settle it for both of them. His father studied that answer for a second longer than the moment strictly needed, then huffed something that was almost a laugh. "Decide everything on your own, tell us only once it's already done. Since you were small, this is how you are." He said it to Mrudula rather than to Ashwin, as though handing her a fact about her new husband that she'd find useful eventually - not a complaint so much as a fond, well-worn observation, the kind a parent only earns the right to make after watching it repeat itself for twenty-odd years. Whatever else Mrudula had braced herself for on that flight - suspicion, an interrogation, the particular chill a runaway bride might reasonably expect from a family meeting her for the first time under these circumstances - none of it arrived. She was simply absorbed, the way a family absorbs someone it has already decided to keep.

The wedding itself happened two days later, small enough that it barely felt like the thing weddings were supposed to be - a priest, a fire, a handful of relatives summoned on short enough notice that half of them arrived still visibly confused about who exactly the bride was. Mrudula wore a saree borrowed from Ashwin's mother, hemmed hastily the night before by a tailor who lived two streets over and asked no questions either. It was over, start to finish, faster than either of them would have believed a wedding could be, and somewhere in the middle of it - she couldn't have said exactly when - Mrudula stopped being a woman who'd run away from something and became, simply, a woman who was married.

---

Her laptop died on the fourth evening of their marriage, mid-charge, the screen going first to a frozen error and then, decisively, to nothing at all.

"It's gone," she said, from the room in his parents' house they were staying in until the two of them found a place of their own, holding it up like evidence. "Two years of coursework notes, gone, unless someone in this city can perform a miracle for less than I can afford."

"Let me look at it."

"You? You can't even work the geyser without help."

"Different skill set." He was already pulling it toward himself, already turning it over to find the access panel with a familiarity that should, in hindsight, have told her something long before it actually did - the way his fingers went straight to the right screws without hunting for them first. Twenty minutes of quiet, focused work later - a cable swapped, something reseated, a command line she didn't recognise flashing past too fast to read, lines of it scrolling with a speed that looked, to her untrained eye, more like fluency than troubleshooting - the screen came back to life, files intact, as though it had never failed at all.

Mrudula stared at it. Then at him.

"How did you just do that."

"It wasn't that hard, actually. Corrupted boot sector, mostly." He said it lightly, already closing the lid, already moving on to something else in the room, the way a person moves past a fact that doesn't feel, to them, like it needs defending.

"Ashwin. That is not a normal MBA student thing to know how to do."

He looked up at that, and something in his face settled into an admission he seemed to have decided, on the spot, was safer given than withheld. "I used to code. Properly, I mean - before the MBA. I was good enough at it that someone offered me a serious job on the strength of it, more money than an MBA was ever going to get me, if I'm honest."

"Then why didn't you take it? Why do an MBA at all?"

"Because I wanted a break from coding." He said it plainly, almost amused at how simple the answer sounded out loud. "Two years of an office chair and other people's bugs will do that to you. I wanted to sit in a classroom and pretend to care about supply chains for a while instead."

She laughed, disbelieving and charmed in roughly equal measure, turning the laptop over once in her hands as though the machine itself might offer a second opinion, and let it go the way a person lets go of an answer that makes just enough sense not to need chasing any further - and if some small, distant part of her filed the moment away regardless, the way she filed most things she didn't have an immediate use for, it didn't so much as flicker across her face that evening.

Ashwin closed the laptop, and didn't mention it again, and neither did she.
