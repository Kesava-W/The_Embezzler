# Chapter Fourteen — Mauritius

The restaurant sat at the top of a short flight of steps off the coast road, the kind of place that did a brisk enough trade on a Friday evening that nobody minded a queue building at the door. Ashwin had his hand at the small of Mrudula's back, nothing more than habit by then, when a group of four came down the steps past them - already loud with the particular confidence of a good dinner and a better bar bill, one of them saying something in Creole-inflected English as his eyes went, deliberately and unhurriedly, over Mrudula from top to bottom.

The word itself mattered less than the laugh that followed it, three of the four joining in before the fourth had properly registered what had even been said.

Mrudula's step didn't falter. Her face didn't change, not in any way a stranger passing on the steps would have caught. Only her hand, briefly, tightened in his.

She kept walking. Ashwin did not immediately follow.

"Go in," he said, easily, like nothing had happened at all. "Order the crab. I'll be two minutes."

"Ashwin -"

"Two minutes." He was already smiling, the ordinary, unbothered smile she'd had no reason to doubt in all the months of knowing him, and it worked exactly as well as it always did - she went in, because there was no version of that smile that looked like anything worth staying outside for.

The door swung shut behind her. The smile went with it.

He didn't move for several seconds - long enough that a passing waiter gave him a second look before deciding whatever was happening on the man's face was none of his business. Something had gone very still and very precise in him, the particular stillness of a man doing arithmetic he didn't want to be doing. When he finally moved, it wasn't toward the restaurant door.

The four of them were still on the steps, in no hurry, waiting on a taxi. Ashwin walked up to the one who'd spoken - the loudest of them, still grinning about his own joke - and extended a hand, easy and open, the kind of gesture that asks a person to reciprocate before their better judgement gets a vote.

"Sorry - couldn't help overhearing. You've got a good sense of humour." Said pleasantly enough that the man, still grinning, not yet sure what was happening, took the offered hand without thinking twice about it.

Ashwin's grip closed the rest of the way. Slowly, at first - the man had time to register only mild surprise at the strength of it. Then not slowly at all.

The grin didn't survive the second it took his knees to understand what was happening before his mind did. He didn't shout. What came out of him was smaller than that and worse for it - a short, strangled sound through his teeth, his free hand scrabbling uselessly at Ashwin's wrist, his face going the particular grey of a man discovering, too late, exactly how badly he has misjudged something.

"You're going to want to be more careful," Ashwin said, still pleasant, still smiling, in a voice pitched for nobody but the man whose hand he was holding, "about who you say things like that in front of."

He let go. The man staggered back into his friends, cradling his hand against his chest, and none of the other three moved to help him - too busy working out exactly what they'd just watched happen, and deciding, wisely, not to test whether it repeated.

Ashwin didn't look back at them again. He straightened his cuff, unhurried, and went inside to order the crab.

It was not, he understood clearly enough even as he did it, anything close to how he did things - not his methods, not his patience, not a single principle that had kept him unremarkable in this country for the better part of a year. He filed the thought away with the same discipline he filed everything else, and by the time he slid into the seat across from Mrudula, nothing in his face suggested he'd filed anything at all.

"Everything alright?" she asked.

"Fine. Ran into someone I went to school with." He picked up the menu. "Small world."

She didn't quite believe him, and let it go the way she let most things go with him.

---

Twenty-six days was long enough, Mrudula had decided somewhere over the Arabian Sea on the flight back, to stop pretending to herself that this was nothing.

She'd texted him from three different airports. *Almost there. Missed you more than I'm going to admit out loud.* His replies had come back short and warm and entirely unlike the guarded first-week version of him she'd once catalogued so carefully in the back of a car - *land safe. I'll be insufferable about this for at least a week, fair warning* - and she'd read that message enough times on the last leg that the woman next to her had started giving her looks. She'd spent the last hour of the flight rehearsing, uselessly, exactly what she'd say when she saw him at the flat, and had arrived at nothing better than *hi*, which she'd decided, somewhere over the coast, was probably going to be enough anyway.

She was still smiling about it when the car turned off the main road toward Black River instead of toward Floréal.

"Papa. This isn't the way home."

"One stop first, beti." Her father didn't turn around. "A family we've wanted you to meet."

"Meet for what."

Her mother answered that one, too carefully, the practised brightness of someone who'd rehearsed this in the mirror. "Just tea. Nothing more than tea."

Mrudula looked between them, the smile from the airport still fading off her face, and something in the careful, coordinated ease of it - the way neither of them had quite looked at her while saying it - told her more than either sentence had.

It was more than tea, and Mrudula understood exactly how much more within thirty seconds of the gate swinging open on a house she recognised from nowhere and immediately understood everything about - the deep verandahs, the sea behind the chair a man was already rising from to greet them, the particular, practised warmth of someone used to being received as important wherever he arrived. A staff member had appeared at the gate before the car had even fully stopped, opening doors, murmuring greetings her father returned with a warmth she hadn't seen him direct at anyone in months.

"Mr and Mrs Pillay." Aadhunik took her father's hand first, then her mother's, and only then turned to Mrudula with a smile that lasted a half-second too long. "And this must be your daughter. They didn't tell me you were this lovely - I would have worn a better shirt."

"You look very well, Mr Aadhunik," Mrudula said, and heard her own voice come out level, pleasant, entirely unlike the sound currently building somewhere under her ribs.

She held that voice through forty minutes of tea she didn't taste, through her father's careful boasting about her MBA - the campus, the cohort, a version of her studies made to sound one rung more impressive than it strictly was - through Aadhunik's questions, easy ones, charming ones, each landing on her like a hand testing whether a door was locked. He asked what she was reading. He asked, lightly, whether Mauritius agreed with her more than she'd expected it to. He mentioned, once, almost in passing, that he didn't much care for the crowd at the business schools these days - too many foreigners, not enough substance - and watched her reaction to it with an attention that made the sentence feel less like small talk than it had any right to. Through her mother's hand finding hers under the table twice, once for reassurance and once, unmistakably, for warning.

"You must stay for dinner," Aadhunik said, at the point in the afternoon when it had stopped being a question anyone at the table felt able to answer honestly. "I insist. And it is a long drive back to Floréal in the dark - my housekeeper can make up a room, your daughter can rest a while before we eat."

Her father accepted before Mrudula had finished deciding how to object, delighted at an evening stretching out exactly the way he'd hoped it might, already asking Aadhunik a follow-up question about land near the coast that had nothing to do with her at all, and by the time the housekeeper showed her up to a guest room at the far end of the house - handsome, unfamiliar, the sea audible through a window she hadn't opened - Mrudula had run out of anywhere left to put the sound building under her ribs.

She sat on the edge of an unfamiliar bed for a long while, phone in hand, typing three different messages to Ashwin and deleting each one before it sent, unable to find a version of the sentence that wasn't going to frighten either of them.

She held it through dinner, three hours of it, her father and Aadhunik deep in a conversation about business and land that required nothing further from her than the occasional smile, her mother filling every silence that threatened to open with a question about the food, the house, anything that kept the table moving. She held it being shown back up to the same guest room afterward, told to rest, her parents settled into a second guest room somewhere on the other side of that enormous house, well past being disturbed by anything short of an alarm bell. She stopped holding it somewhere around midnight, alone in that unfamiliar room, face down into a pillow so the sound wouldn't carry.

---

She didn't hear the window at first. By the time she registered it, Ashwin was already through it, salt-damp at the cuffs, moving with a quiet that struck her, in the half-second before relief overtook everything else, as slightly too practised for a man who'd had to come up from the shore side of a guarded estate he'd never once mentioned knowing his way around.

"You scared the life out of me -"

"You texted me you were stuck at some stranger's dinner and then went quiet for three hours." He was already crossing the room to her, and whatever had been careful and quiet about the climb was gone from him now. "I got worried."

She didn't slap him for the fright. She kissed him instead, hard, both hands fisted in his shirt, twenty-six days and one unbearable afternoon collapsing into it at once, and for a moment the house around them - the sleeping parents, the man downstairs, the whole engineered afternoon - simply ceased to exist.

Later - she couldn't have said how much later, only that the room had gone soft and dark and unhurried around them - he lay with his head in her lap, and she ran her fingers slowly through his hair, and for a while neither of them said anything that needed saying. Outside, the sea kept up its steady, indifferent sound against the shore below the house, close enough to hear and too far to see.

"I have to tell you something," she said finally, into the quiet. "And I need you not to say anything until I've finished, because if you say anything I won't get through it."

He went still under her hand. "Okay."

She told him. The house in Black River. The tea she hadn't tasted. The name Aadhunik, said out loud for the first time since the car ride, and everything her parents hadn't said but had made unmistakably clear regardless - the questions posed like a man taking inventory, her father's delight at every answer she gave, the particular way the whole afternoon had felt less like a visit than an audition she hadn't known she was giving.

"They want this, Ashwin. Not tomorrow, not urgently, but they want it, and I don't know how to make that stop wanting it without breaking something in my own family that I don't know how to fix afterward." Her voice had started to fray somewhere in the middle of that sentence and she pushed through it anyway. "I don't know what to do. I don't know what I'm supposed to do."

He sat up. Slowly, deliberately, the way a man sits up when he's already decided what he's about to say and is only buying himself the last few seconds before he has to say it.

"Maybe," he said, "we should reconsider our relationship."

The room went very quiet.

"Reconsider." She said the word back to him like it belonged to a language she'd only just started learning. "You want me to - Ashwin, I just told you my parents are trying to marry me off to a stranger, and your answer is that we should reconsider -"

"I'm saying it might be safer -"

"Don't." Her hand was already moving before the rest of her had caught up with it, the slap landing before either of them fully understood it was happening, and the silence that followed was worse than the sound had been. "Don't you dare tell me what's safer. I came in here and told you the worst thing that's happened to me in a year and you're already halfway out the door -"

The door didn't open quietly.

---

Aadhunik filled the doorway the way he filled every room in his own house, except that every softness the tea table had carried that afternoon was gone from his face entirely. A man who checked on his own grounds most nights out of habit more than suspicion had, this once, had reason to.

"Who is this."

Mrudula was on her feet. "Aadhunik, please, it isn't -"

"I am not asking for an explanation." He hadn't raised his voice yet. That was, somehow, worse than if he had. "I am asking who is in a guest's bedroom, in my own house, at midnight."

"I'm not -" Ashwin started, and got no further than that.

It happened fast, and it happened badly. Aadhunik crossed the room in three strides and the first blow landed before Ashwin had finished turning to face him properly - a crack of knuckle against jaw, the sound of it wrong in a way that made Mrudula's whole body flinch - and after that there was no more room in it for anything that resembled a fight. Only Aadhunik, and the wall, and Ashwin absorbing what came without once raising a hand to stop it, which read, to anyone watching, as a man too frightened or too broken to defend himself, and read as nothing else at all.

A fist. Then another. The back of a hand.

"Aadhunik, stop -" Mrudula was pulling at an arm that didn't move for her, might as well have been pulling at the wall itself.

He didn't stop. Each blow came harder than the last, less controlled, the particular rhythm of a man who has stopped calculating and started simply needing this to go on, and somewhere in the middle of it Mrudula understood, with a clarity that cut straight through the panic, that this was no longer a beating that intended to end with Ashwin conscious. A lamp went over. Glass, somewhere. Ashwin's back against the wardrobe, sliding, one eye already closing.

She got between them. Not gracefully - she half-fell into the space, one hand flat against Aadhunik's chest, the other still gripping the arm that had nearly thrown her aside a second before.

"STOP." Her voice broke on the word and she didn't care. "Please - please, I'm begging you, stop, I'll do anything, just stop -"

He stopped.

He stood over both of them, breathing hard, one hand still closed into a fist that hadn't yet decided to open, and when he spoke again his voice had gone flat and terribly calm, the calm of a man who has just remembered he has leverage rather than only rage.

"You will end this," he said, "tonight, in front of me, right now, or I finish what I started and nobody in Mauritius asks me a single question about it."

Mrudula looked down at Ashwin - at what was left of his face turned toward her, one eye already swelling shut, no fight left in him that she could see, nothing but the specific, terrible patience of a man waiting to find out what she would say next.

"We're -" Her voice gave out entirely and she had to start again, forcing each word past something closing in her throat. "We're over. It's finished. We're not - we were never anything. It's done."

She said it looking at Ashwin the whole time, not at Aadhunik, and the tears running down her face while she said it made every word of it sound exactly as false as it was - which didn't matter, in the end, to the only person in the room whose satisfaction the sentence had actually been built for.
