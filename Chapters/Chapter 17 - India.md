# Chapter Seventeen — India

The bungalow sat off a disused lane in Rajokri, one of the old farmhouse plots the city had grown up around rather than through, its boundary wall high enough that the traffic on the bypass beyond it was the only sign Delhi still existed on the other side. A single approach road, unlit for the last half-kilometre. Dogs somewhere beyond the wall that had stopped barking hours ago, having long since worked out that nothing came down that lane worth the effort.

Sixty of them, by the final count - NGO heads who had watched a year and a half of their peers turn up dead or ruined and had decided, each in his own private arithmetic, that the safest place left to be was inside a wall this thick, surrounded by men this well paid. Phones switched off for a week, every one of them, on the collective theory that a phone was the only thing that had ever gotten anyone killed - stacked, at the first night's insistence, in a single locked drawer nobody was meant to have the key to, though at least four of them had quietly kept a second phone the others didn't know about. The bulk of their money - crores of it, cash and gold between them - sat in a reinforced room at the back of the house, watched over by goons hired on a promise of lifetime settlement that made the guarding feel, to the men doing it, like the safest work they'd ever taken. The remaining twenty crore sat somewhere far less physical, in an account whose details only one of the sixty actually held, a precaution that felt, on the evening it was made, like the smartest thing any of them had done. There had been card games in the drawing room the first two nights, and a kind of forced good cheer that only thinly papered over the fact that sixty grown men, some of whom had run their own trusts for decades, were sleeping four to a room because none of them trusted a hallway alone.

It was Guddu who took the call, a little after midnight, phone left on against every instruction because a man guarding sixty crores does not, in practice, switch off the one thing that lets him call for help.

"Boss." He didn't recognise the number. He answered anyway. "Who is this?"

"Somebody who admires how well you're doing your job." The voice was flattened, even, patient in a way that made Guddu's arm hair rise before his mind caught up with why. "Sixty men trusting you with everything they own. That is not nothing. Most men in your line of work never get handed that kind of respect."

"What do you want."

"Nothing from you tonight. I'm calling because I think you should know - these sixty men you're guarding? They are going to be broke by morning either way. Every account, every rupee that isn't sitting in that room with you, already gone, already decided, nothing you or I can do to stop it." A pause, unhurried. "I only called because it felt unfair that you wouldn't know. A man deserves to know what he's actually guarding."

Guddu put it on speaker without entirely deciding to, the way a man reaches for a second opinion he doesn't know he needs until the room is already listening.

The others gathered fast, six or seven of them drifting in from the corridor in vests and undone shirts, one still holding a half-finished cup of tea he never drank the rest of, and the caller let the silence sit long enough for them to start filling it themselves.

"If it's all going to zero anyway," one of them said, low, half to the room and half to himself, "then what were we even promised."

"Lifetime settlement," another one said, and laughed, short and ugly. "From sixty men who are about to have nothing left to settle anyone with."

"They're old men," a third put in. "Half of them can't climb a flight of stairs without stopping. You've seen them."

"They pay us to sit outside doors while they play cards," said a fourth, quieter, though not quiet enough that the others didn't hear it. "For that we get a settlement that's about to be worth nothing."

The caller didn't answer any of it directly. When he spoke again, it was a question, mild, almost bored. "If you knew - and I'm only saying if - that the men in that house are weak enough that none of them could stop you, and that everything they own besides the room you're already guarding is going to vanish regardless of what any of you do tonight... what would you do?"

Nobody answered him out loud. They didn't need to. Guddu looked at the men around him, and the men around him looked back, and something passed between all of them that none of them would ever be able to describe afterward as a decision, exactly, more a permission finally granted for a thought several of them had been quietly having since the caller's second sentence. Someone's hand had already found the grip of a weapon that had, until that moment, been purely for show.

The call had already ended by the time the first gun came out.

It went fast after that, and it went badly for almost everyone in the house. Sixty men who had trusted a wall and a guard roster over anything resembling their own readiness had nothing to answer thirty armed men with - not a plan, not a weapon between most of them, only the particular, useless courage of people realising too late what the evening actually was. A shout. A door kicked in. Then another. Voices raised in confusion before they understood there was nothing left to be confused about. Doors kicked in. Shouting that turned, room by room, into something with no more shouting left in it. The bungalow's long central corridor became the worst place in the house to be standing within the first two minutes, and most of them were standing in it.

Deepak Netam was not standing in it.

Netam's Van Adhikar Manch ran forest-rights cases in the Bastar belt on paper, and had done, for eleven years now, ever since Netam himself had walked out of the forest under a state surrender scheme and traded a rifle for a registration certificate. The paper was the point. Behind the case files and the foreign grant money that funded them sat a second ledger nobody at the registrar's office had ever seen - old comrades never quite let go of, a cut of every donation still finding its way back to the networks he'd supposedly surrendered out of, the same unrest he'd once fought for now funded at arm's length instead of at gunpoint. He had never once believed in his own reform, not for a single one of the eleven years since, which was exactly why he'd never believed a promise of safety either - a guard roster and a locked room instead of an exit route was precisely the kind of promise he'd made to other men himself, once, and broken whenever the cause required it. He knew exactly what a fortress mentality was worth. He'd taken a rear room instead of a central one. He'd kept, against every rule the gathering had set for itself, an old AK-47 he still knew how to strip in the dark, wrapped inside a bedroll, the kind of precaution the other fifty-nine had mocked him for, gently, over dinner the first night.

He came out of that rear room when the shooting reached his corridor, and for close to ninety seconds, he was the only person in that bungalow still fighting rather than dying.

"Netam!" A voice down the hall, one of the other heads, alive and pinned behind an overturned table. "Netam, here, cover this side -"

He didn't answer with words. A single controlled burst. Then another, shorter. Three of Guddu's men went down before they understood where the return fire was even coming from, and for those ninety seconds the whole shape of the night threatened to bend somewhere else entirely - enough that two of the goons broke and ran for the outer wall rather than press forward into a corridor that had stopped being one-directional.

Reload. A beat of nothing. Then fire again.

It wasn't enough. Thirty against one, even one who'd survived exactly this kind of arithmetic before, only ever ended one way, and Netam's rifle went quiet a little after the two men reached the wall - not a second surrender, this time, only the simple fact that there was nothing left in the house for him to be defending.

By the time it was over, the corridor and every room off it held more bodies than the bungalow's original sixty had accounted for, goons among them now too, the ratio nowhere near as one-sided as thirty armed men should have made it. The card table in the drawing room still stood upright, cards scattered across it exactly where the last hand had been abandoned. The cash room stood untouched at the back of the house, doors still locked, nobody left alive with any reason left to open them.

The twenty crore went the same night, quietly, without a single alarm tripped, into an account that would prove, when anyone finally thought to check it, exactly as untraceable as every other account this same operation had already emptied.

The police found it all the next morning - a groundskeeper's call, then uniforms, then considerably more uniforms than Rajokri's local station usually sent anywhere, a line of vehicles backed up along the unlit lane by mid-morning that the dogs beyond the wall had, by then, given up barking at entirely. Nobody who arrived that morning found a single rupee out of place in the room at the back of the house. Nobody found the twenty crore either, not that morning, not for a long time after. What they found instead was a bungalow that would take three days simply to finish counting, and a story that would run for a single news cycle, gang violence, motive unclear, before the country's attention moved on to whatever came next, the way it always did.
