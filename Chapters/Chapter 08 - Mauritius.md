# Chapter Eight — Mauritius

The call came in before the news bulletin did, which was usually the way it went with anything involving Aadhunik Marine Freight.

Vinesh Deelchand, who ran the company's day-to-day out of the Port Louis office and had done for six years without a single call to Aadhunik that started well, did not waste time on the preamble. Customs and the Anti-Drug Smuggling Unit had run a joint check on two outbound containers that morning, the kind of routine check that happened perhaps once in twenty sailings, and this had been the once - a dog handler working the queue out of boredom more than instinct, by all accounts, until the dog stopped being bored. Eleven kilos, packed into false flooring under a shipment of frozen fish, two of the company's own loading staff caught standing close enough to it that "unaware" wasn't going to be a defence anybody's lawyer could make stick. Under the Act, straight resale of that quantity carried up to fifteen years, and the two men were already in an ADSU holding cell by the time Deelchand got Aadhunik on the phone.

Aadhunik heard the whole thing out in silence, which anyone who knew him would have told you was worse than shouting. Deelchand kept talking into that silence longer than he meant to - the container number, the shift roster, the name of the ADSU officer who'd signed the seizure report - because a silence like that needed filling with something, and facts were the only thing he had to offer it.

"Fifteen years," Aadhunik said finally, not really to Deelchand. "For two loaders. And they will make it my company, my name, on every bulletin tonight."

"Sir, if we distance ourselves publicly, say it was these two men acting alone -"

"You think this is about two men?" His voice had found its register now, low and even and considerably more dangerous than a raised one would have been. "You think the government cares about eleven kilos of ganja? They have been looking for something - anything - since that policeman came to my house with his begging bowl. Now they have their something." He was already moving toward the study, the phone still at his ear, a man rehearsing an argument he intended to have with people who weren't on the call. "Every licence I hold, every permit, every container that leaves that harbour, they will crawl over it now, looking for the next eleven kilos, and it will not matter to a single one of them whether I knew, whether I sanctioned it, whether I have ever once dealt in that filth myself. They have decided who I am. This was always going to happen."

"The two men - will you want a lawyer sent, or -"

"Send one. Not for their sake." A pause, the sound of ice against glass somewhere in it. "For mine. I do not want either of them saying something interesting to a bored policeman at three in the morning."

Deelchand did not point out that two of the company's own loaders had, in fact, packed eleven kilograms of cannabis into a shipping container under Aadhunik Marine Freight's own paperwork, because it was not a point that was going to land anywhere useful that evening. He said he'd see to the lawyer, and that he would call again if anything changed, and Aadhunik had already hung up before the second sentence finished.

By the nine o'clock bulletin the story had exactly the shape Aadhunik had predicted - his company's name in the anchor's mouth before the word "arrest" was, footage of the ADSU van outside the Port Louis depot filmed at an angle that made the whole building look guiltier than a warehouse had any right to, a junior minister given thirty seconds to say the government would not tolerate this on Mauritian soil, delivered with the flat, over-rehearsed cadence of a man reading a line he hadn't written. In the kitchen, two floors below where Aadhunik sat, the household staff watched the same segment on a smaller set with the volume low, and nobody said anything either, for entirely different reasons.

Aadhunik watched the whole segment from the same chair he always took, the sea dark behind him, one hand around a glass he wasn't drinking from. He said nothing further about the two men currently facing fifteen years for a decision that had nothing to do with him and everything to do with a name he happened to share with the company. When the bulletin moved on to a story about a flooded canal in Rose Hill, he did not change the channel. He simply stopped watching it, the way a man stops listening to rain once it's made clear it isn't going to stop.

---

It was the housekeeper's nephew, a boy of perhaps nineteen who'd been brought on for the season to help with the grounds, who was caught two days later with his hand still inside Aadhunik's wallet, left carelessly on the hall table the way it sometimes was when Aadhunik trusted a room more than he should have.

He didn't run. There wasn't anywhere to run to, not on a property with a gate and a guard on it who knew his face and would only have to say one word into a radio, and some instinct in him seemed to understand that running would only make whatever came next worse. He stood there instead, the wallet still in his hand, two notes half out of it, close enough to the door that he could hear the sea and far enough from it that the distance had already stopped mattering. He'd rehearsed, in the two or three seconds it took Aadhunik to cross the hall, a version of events involving a dropped coin and a wallet that had simply fallen open - and abandoned it before it reached his mouth, because Aadhunik's face made clear the story wasn't going to be needed.

Aadhunik looked at him for a long moment. Long enough that the boy's hand had started, visibly, to shake, the two notes trembling loose another half-inch out of the leather. Long enough that somewhere behind Aadhunik, a member of the household staff who'd rounded the corner into the hall took one look and quietly withdrew the way she'd come, wanting no part of whatever was about to happen in that space.

"Put it back," Aadhunik said. "And go and help your aunt with the linen."

The boy didn't move at first, certain he had misheard, certain this was some longer, crueller version of the thing he'd been bracing for - a game before the real punishment, the kind rich men sometimes played with people who couldn't afford to walk away from it. When Aadhunik simply turned and walked back toward the verandah, unhurried, already reaching for the drink he'd left on the table, the boy put the notes back into the wallet with hands that hadn't yet stopped shaking, closed it, set it down exactly where he'd found it, and went to find his aunt.

He told her nothing. Not that evening, not in the days that followed, though she noticed - the way aunts do - that something in him had gone quiet for a week. There was nothing about it he could have explained even if he'd tried, no version of the story that made sense once he tried to lay it out in order: a man capable of watching two innocent employees absorb a fifteen-year sentence without a flicker, who had, for no reason the boy could name, simply let him go.

The following morning, on the same hall table, an extra note sat folded once beneath the wallet's usual spot - not tucked inside it, not handed to anyone, just left there in a way that could, if anyone asked, have been mistaken for something dropped by accident. Nobody asked. The boy found it before his aunt did, and said nothing about that either.

Nobody in that house, least of all the boy, ever learned why.
