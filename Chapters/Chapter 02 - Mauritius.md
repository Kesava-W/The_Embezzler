# Chapter Two — Mauritius

Six thousand kilometres from that Delhi committee room, on the dry side of an island that measured itself in minutes rather than kilometres, none of it had reached anyone yet.

The Black River coast ran drier than the rest of Mauritius, sheltered from the trade winds by the central plateau, and by mid-morning the light off the water had already gone hard and white. Aadhunik's house sat above the road on reclaimed cane land, colonial in the bones of it - deep verandahs, a roof pitched steep against rain that rarely came this side of the island - and modern in everything the money had touched since: a glass extension facing the sea, a security gate manned around the clock, a private jetty at the bottom of the garden where a boat that never seemed to fish sat under its cover.

The car that came up the drive that morning was not one that belonged there. White, government-plated, air-conditioning labouring against the heat before it had even stopped. The guard at the gate had waved it through with the particular carefulness of a man who'd been told, in advance, not to make a scene of it.

Assistant Commissioner of Police Kevin Appadoo had worn his uniform. Not because the visit called for it - it didn't, not officially, there was no warrant in his hand and no case number attached to what he was about to say - but because a man his rank does not knock on a door like this in plain clothes and expect to be taken seriously, or to be believed later, by anyone above him, that he'd said what he came to say. He'd spent the drive up rehearsing three different versions of the same three sentences, discarding the first for sounding like a threat and the second for sounding like an apology, and had settled, by the time the gate came into view, on something he hoped landed in the narrow space between the two. He was thirty-eight years old. He had a daughter who'd started asking, that year, what her father actually did all day, and he had not yet found an honest answer that didn't sound, to his own ears, faintly ridiculous.

Aadhunik received him on the verandah, in the chair he always took, the one with the sea behind it so that anyone sitting opposite had the sun in their eyes and he didn't. He didn't stand when Appadoo was shown out. He gestured, instead, at the chair across from him - an offer, apparently genuine, of the seat Appadoo had already been told he wouldn't be extended.

"Sit, sit. You've come a long way for a Tuesday." There was, for those first few seconds, something almost welcoming in it, a host's reflex operating a beat ahead of whatever this visit actually was. "Coffee? I have a woman who makes it properly, not this instant nonsense."

"I won't be staying that long, Monsieur Aadhunik." Appadoo did not sit. "I have been asked to convey something to you directly, rather than in writing."

"Convey."

"There is a view, at a certain level, that certain arrangements of yours - shipments, accounts, the company's dealings on the coast here and further out - would be best wound down. Voluntarily. Before this becomes something neither of us wants it to become." He had rehearsed this in the car; it showed in how evenly it came out, each clause landing where he'd planned it to, a man reciting rather than speaking. "I am asking you, on the government's behalf, to consider that."

For a moment Aadhunik only looked at him, and there was something almost warm in it, the beginning of a smile Appadoo couldn't immediately place as one thing or another. Some part of Appadoo - the part that had, against his own better judgement, read enough about this man to half-admire the sheer scale of what he'd built from a licence and some nerve - wondered, in that half-second, whether this might actually go the easy way.

Then Aadhunik laughed. Not loudly. A single exhale through the nose, the kind that costs a man nothing and is meant to cost the other one everything.

"You came all this way," Aadhunik said, "in a government car, in that uniform, to ask me nicely."

"Monsieur -"

"Who sent you. Say the name."

Appadoo didn't. The pause did the work instead, and Aadhunik read it the way he read most silences directed at him - as confirmation that whoever had sent this man hadn't wanted their own name anywhere near the room. The warmth was gone from his face by then. What replaced it moved fast: amusement first, curdling almost before it finished forming, into something flatter and colder that had been sitting underneath it the whole time, the coffee offer already a thing that had happened to a different, more patient version of this same conversation.

"Get off my property."

"Sir, I would ask you to understand this is -"

The rest of it never came. Aadhunik was out of the chair before Appadoo had finished the sentence, and the slap landed open-handed, hard enough to turn Appadoo's head, hard enough that the sound of it carried to the two staff members clearing glasses by the far door, who did not look up, because looking up was its own kind of risk in this house. One of them, the younger one, flinched at the sound the way a person flinches at something they've heard before and hoped, each time, not to hear again.

Appadoo didn't touch his own face. He straightened his collar instead, a small, deliberate correction, and said nothing further. There was nothing further that would do any good, and some part of him - the part that had sat in the car rehearsing, the part that had worn the uniform for exactly this reason - had already known that before he came up the drive. What surprised him, walking back to the car with his cheek still warm, wasn't the slap itself. It was how little anger he actually found in himself underneath it - mostly just a tired, professional certainty that he'd file this exactly the way the last two officers before him had filed the same encounter, in a drawer that existed for precisely this purpose and would go on existing for a good while yet.

He left the way he'd arrived: unhurried, the guard opening the gate for him with the same careful non-expression as before, the government car pulling back onto the coast road as though nothing about the last four minutes had happened at all. He was not the first officer to make this drive and leave it feeling like this. He would not, though nobody on that verandah knew it yet, be the last time this house saw him either.

Aadhunik was back in his chair before the car had cleared the gate, the sea behind him, the sting already fading from his palm faster than it had from Appadoo's face. One of the staff came forward to ask, in Kreol, whether Missie wanted the coffee brought out now or later - the same coffee, still, that he'd offered a policeman two minutes earlier as though the offer had cost him nothing to make and nothing to withdraw - and he said now, and that was the entire weight the morning was ever going to carry with him. There had been others before Appadoo - a different uniform, a different car, the same request phrased a different way - and every one of them had ended exactly like this: a raised voice, a closed gate, and Aadhunik's attention returning, within minutes, to whichever number on whichever spreadsheet had held it before the interruption.

Nothing in the last four minutes had cost him anything he could feel by afternoon.
